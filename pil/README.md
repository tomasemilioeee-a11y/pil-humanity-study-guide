# Public International Law

**Exam:** Mon 30 Nov · 09:00–12:00 · USC Hall 2  
**Rules:** Closed book, typed on a university Chromebook with no internet. Only a clean hardcopy of Blackstone's International Law Documents (16th ed.) is allowed. Covers all weeks, 1–11 (the syllabus said 7–11; you confirmed the final covers everything). Know the cases and apply them; paragraph numbers are not needed.  
**Format:** Expect a layered fictional fact pattern asking you to advise a State (or clerk for an ICJ judge), in the style of the course's sample questions: Azargh and Rio del Oro (weeks 2 and 4), Jalapenia, Assad in Berlin, the EU raw-materials mock, Valdoria/Ironwatch (weeks 7–10). Answer in dependency order: who is the actor (State, people, IO) → which source binds (treaty, custom) → is it valid, in force, reserved, interpreted how → was it breached and attributable → excuses → consequences and invocation.

## Answer method

1. Heading and a one-line bottom line: 'Memorandum to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of X. In short: …'
2. Issue map, numbered as questions in dependency order. Split your words in proportion to the points per sub-question.
3. Each issue in IRAC: rule with its source (Blackstone article number plus the case), application using every fact given, the other side's best argument and your rebuttal, mini-conclusion. Marks come in 1–2 point units per correct proposition, so state each step in its own sentence.
4. If a treaty route fails (non-party, termination, reservation), ask whether a customary rule with the same content still binds (VCLT Art 43; Nicaragua paras 175–179).
5. Remedies and next steps: cessation, reparation, countermeasures, forum (ICJ jurisdiction).
6. One or two sentences of critical reflection for originality marks (Eurocentrism, judicial activism in custom-finding, fragmentation).
7. Tab Blackstone: UN Charter (Arts 1, 2, 4, 25, 39–51, 103), ICJ Statute (Arts 36, 38, 59), VCLT (Arts 2, 7, 11–21, 26–27, 31–33, 34–38, 42–64), Montevideo Convention, GA Res 1514, 1541, 2625, 3314, ARSIWA, Rome Statute, UNCSI.

## Practice problems

Original problems in the style of the exam. Write under time first, then open the checklist.

### The Varnia border strikes (75 min)

Varnia and Ostrel are UN members and parties to the ICJ Statute, and both have accepted compulsory jurisdiction under Art 36(2) without reservations. For two years the Liberation Front of Eastern Ostrel (LFEO), an armed separatist group, has operated from camps in northern Varnia. Varnia's intelligence service paid LFEO salaries, provided rifles and drones, and a Varnian colonel sat on the LFEO 'strategic council', which sets annual objectives. In March, LFEO fighters crossed into Ostrel and shelled a town, killing 40 civilians. Varnia's President praised the attack on television as 'a legitimate act of liberation that Varnia fully stands behind'. Two weeks later Ostrel launched air strikes on LFEO camps and on a Varnian army depot 50 km from the border, without reporting to the Security Council. Ostrel also froze the assets of Varnia's central bank and suspended the bilateral air services treaty. Varnia says the strikes were aggression and that Ostrel must pay compensation. Advise Ostrel.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Did the LFEO attack amount to an armed attack? Consider scale and effects (Nicaragua), and whether one incident or an accumulation (Oil Platforms).
- Attribution of LFEO conduct to Varnia: Art 4 de facto organ (complete dependence? likely not); Art 8 effective control over the March operation (the colonel on the strategic council suggests general, not operation-specific, control; Tadić overall control is not the ICJ test); Art 11 acknowledgment and adoption by the President's statement ('fully stands behind', compare Tehran Hostages).
- Indirect armed attack through Res 3314 Art 3(g): 'sending' or 'substantial involvement'. Is the support enough under Nicaragua?
- If not attributable, can Ostrel act in self-defence against a non-state actor on Varnian territory? Wall para 139 and DRC v Uganda versus the 'unwilling or unable' practice. Varnia's due diligence breach (Corfu Channel).
- Necessity and proportionality: camps versus the depot 50 km away. Did the depot contribute to the attack (Oil Platforms target analysis)?
- The failure to report under Art 51: not fatal, but evidential weight (Nicaragua para 200).
- Asset freeze and treaty suspension as countermeasures: a prior wrong by Varnia; proportionality; Art 52 procedure; the central bank's immunity from execution; Air Service.
- Remedies and forum: Art 36(2) jurisdiction; Varnia's counterclaims; reparation (Arts 31–37).

</details>

### The minister in Kelland (60 min)

General Dorn was Minister of Defence of Azeria until January. During his tenure Azerian forces tortured detainees from the Mirani minority, some of them nationals of Kelland. In February Dorn became Azeria's Foreign Minister. In June, while on an official visit to Kelland for a climate conference, he was arrested on a warrant issued by a Kellandic investigating judge for torture and crimes against humanity, based on Kelland's universal jurisdiction law and the Kellandic nationality of several victims. Separately, Mirani survivors are suing Azeria in Kelland's civil courts for damages, and the court has ordered the seizure of an Azerian State-owned airline's aircraft at Kelland airport. Kelland and Azeria are parties to the CAT. Azeria demands Dorn's immediate release and the lifting of the seizure. Advise Kelland.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Bases of prescriptive jurisdiction: universal jurisdiction (CAT Arts 5 and 7; custom for CAH); passive personality (Kellandic victims); the presence of the accused at the time of arrest; Lotus versus the requirement of a permissive basis.
- Immunity ratione personae of the incumbent Foreign Minister: Arrest Warrant applies directly. There is no exception for international crimes before foreign national courts, and arrest violates inviolability.
- Acts done as Defence Minister: functional immunity would matter after office (Pinochet; ILC Draft Art 7), but now personal immunity bars everything.
- Arrest Warrant para 61 alternatives: prosecution in Azeria, waiver, after office, international courts (ICC if it has jurisdiction, Art 27).
- Civil claims against Azeria: State immunity for acta jure imperii (torture by armed forces); Germany v Italy; Al-Adsani; the territorial tort exception doesn't apply (acts in Azeria).
- Seizure of the airline aircraft: immunity from execution is separate and stricter. Property must be in commercial use and connected to the entity (UN Convention Arts 19–21). Is a State-owned airline a separate entity?
- Consequences: release, cessation, possible satisfaction; Kelland's obligations under CAT Art 7 (aut dedere aut judicare, Belgium v Senegal) versus immunity.

</details>

### Drought, a dam and a trade ban (60 min)

Under a 1995 treaty, Lacoria must release 40% of the River Sel's flow to downstream Brava. In 2026 an extreme drought, partly worsened by Lacoria's own decade of excessive irrigation, threatened Lacoria's capital with water shortages. Lacoria cut releases to 10% for eight months. Brava's farmers lost their harvest. In response, Brava (a) banned imports of all Lacorian agricultural products, claiming they are produced with water 'stolen' from Brava, and (b) refused to repay a sovereign loan instalment owed to Lacoria. Both are WTO members. Brava says Lacoria must restore the flow and pay for the lost harvest; Lacoria says it acted out of necessity and that Brava's ban violates the GATT. Advise both sides briefly, then give your view.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Breach: reduced releases are a breach of the treaty obligation, attributable as State organ conduct (Art 4).
- Necessity (Art 25): an essential interest (the capital's water supply) and grave and imminent peril (likely met). 'Only way'? (alternatives such as rationing and other sources). No serious impairment of Brava's essential interest? Contribution through irrigation (Gabčíkovo; CMS). It probably fails because of contribution.
- Force majeure (Art 23)? The drought is an external event, but performance wasn't materially impossible and Lacoria contributed.
- Art 27: compensation even if necessity succeeded; the duty to resume releases.
- Reparation: cessation and restitution of flow; compensation for lost harvest (Art 36, Chorzów); contributory fault arguments (Art 39).
- Brava's import ban: GATT Art XI violation; Art XX(b) or (g) (water as an exhaustible resource? the nexus); the chapeau; a ban on all products is over-broad. Can trade measures be countermeasures within the WTO (exclusive DSU, Art 23 DSU)?
- Refusal to repay the loan as a countermeasure: a prior wrong; proportionality; reversibility; Art 52 notification; Air Service.

</details>

### Standing for the Rokai (45 min)

Security forces in Tessia are carrying out mass killings and forced deportations of the Rokai, an ethnic minority. A UN fact-finding mission finds 'reasonable grounds' that genocidal intent may be present. Delmar, a small State with no Rokai nationals and no ties to Tessia, wants to bring a case before the ICJ under the Genocide Convention (both are parties, and Tessia has no reservation to Art IX). Delmar also wants to impose a ban on Tessian State officials' assets and suspend a bilateral investment treaty with Tessia. Advise Delmar on (1) its standing and what it can ask for, and (2) whether its unilateral measures are lawful.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Erga omnes partes obligations under the Genocide Convention (prevent and punish), and the common interest of all parties: Belgium v Senegal; Gambia v Myanmar.
- Art 48 ARSIWA: a non-injured State may claim cessation, assurances, and reparation in the interest of the beneficiaries (the Rokai).
- Provisional measures: plausibility of rights, urgency, irreparable harm (Gambia v Myanmar; South Africa v Israel).
- Serious breach of a peremptory norm (Arts 40–41): duties of non-recognition, non-assistance, and cooperation to end it.
- Asset ban on officials: may be retorsion if lawful in itself; check the immunity of officials' and State property.
- BIT suspension as a third-State countermeasure: Art 54 ('lawful measures') and the debate (State practice on Russia sanctions); Art 50 limits; proportionality; alternatively treaty suspension under VCLT Art 60(1)-(2) for material breach? (Not applicable: Art 60 only covers breach of the same treaty, and the breach is of the Genocide Convention, not the BIT. Art 60(5) is the separate rule that humanitarian provisions may not be suspended.)

</details>

### The Republic of Esmera and the Lake Varo treaty (60 min)

In 2019 the eastern province of the State of Moria declared independence as the Republic of Esmera after a referendum boycotted by the non-Esmeran minority. Neighbouring Tarsis, whose troops had entered the province weeks before the referendum 'to protect ethnic Tarsians', recognised Esmera immediately; eleven other States followed by 2024. Esmera has a functioning administration, but its budget, currency and border security depend on Tarsis. In 2021 Esmera's Deputy Minister of Water signed a 'Memorandum on Lake Varo' with Moria's Ministry of Environment, under which each side 'shall' limit irrigation withdrawals to 30% of inflow; it states it 'enters into force upon signature' and was registered by Moria with the UN Secretariat. Esmera's constitution requires parliamentary approval for international agreements, which was never sought, and Esmera never protested the registration. In 2023 Esmera acceded to the 1997 UN Watercourses Convention with a reservation 'excluding Article 7 (no significant harm) in its entirety'; Moria objected but did not oppose the Convention's entry into force between them. In 2025, during a drought, Esmera tripled withdrawals and announced it was 'terminating' the Memorandum because of 'a fundamental change in climatic circumstances'. Moria asks whether Esmera is bound and has breached its obligations. Advise Moria.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Is Esmera a State? Montevideo criteria; effective government versus dependence on Tarsis (independence; puppet-State analogies: Manchukuo, TRNC); legality — creation linked to Tarsis's use of force and a contested referendum (SC Res 541 TRNC analogy; Kosovo para 81); recognition by 12 States is evidence only (declaratory theory); Tarsis's recognition possibly premature and unlawful intervention. Consequence: if not a State, the Memorandum may not be a treaty under the VCLT (but could still bind as an agreement governed by IL with a limited subject).
- Self-determination: are Esmerans a 'people' entitled to external self-determination? Not colonial or occupied; internal self-determination; Badinter and Quebec require parent consent; remedial secession contested.
- If a State: is the Memorandum a treaty? Form and intent ('shall', entry into force on signature, registration, no protest — Qatar v Bahrain; Somalia v Kenya).
- Authority (Art 7): a deputy minister lacks ex officio capacity — full powers? Subsequent confirmation (Art 8). Internal law: parliamentary approval not sought — Art 46 only if manifest and fundamental (Cameroon v Nigeria; Somalia v Kenya); acquiescence (Art 45).
- Reservation excluding Art 7 of the Watercourses Convention: compatible with object and purpose (Art 19(c))? Effects of Moria's simple objection (Art 21(3)); an invalid reservation (ILC Guide 4.5.3). And the no-harm rule exists in custom anyway (Guide 4.4.2; Pulp Mills; Climate AO due diligence).
- Termination for fundamental change (Art 62): drought foreseeable? essential basis? radical transformation? Gabčíkovo para 104 sets a very high bar; procedure (Arts 65–67) not followed. Necessity does not terminate (Gabčíkovo).
- Breach and remedies: tripling withdrawals breaches the 30% cap; State responsibility; possible necessity defence (Art 25) fails if Esmera contributed or other means existed; Moria may claim cessation and reparation, consider countermeasures, and seize the ICJ if jurisdiction exists.

</details>

## Practice essay questions

1. 'Article 2(4) is dead.' Assess this claim with reference to recent uses of force (2022–2026) and the three responses discussed in the Week 7 lecture. Does it matter?
2. Does the ICJ's finding in Nicaragua that there is 'no general right of intervention' in support of an opposition still reflect international law today?
3. The ICJ and the ICTY disagree on the test for attributing the conduct of armed groups (Nicaragua and Bosnia v Tadić). Does this disagreement fragment international law, and which test should prevail?
4. Can domestic litigation over arms exports (CAAT v Secretary of State) be more effective than the ARSIWA in preventing State complicity in violations of international law?
5. Is immunity from execution of State property (Philippine Embassy, Ascom, seizures of Russian assets) an obstacle to effective remedies for victims?
6. Critically assess whether the 'effective control' test is still fit for purpose for attributing the conduct of armed groups to States.
7. 'The ICJ's case law on self-defence against non-state actors is out of step with State practice.' Discuss.
8. Does the procedural nature of immunity, as explained in Germany v Italy, adequately reconcile State immunity with jus cogens?
9. Are security exceptions in international economic law (GATT Art XXI and investment treaty clauses) an acceptable safety valve or a loophole?
10. Explain how countermeasures function as a mechanism for decentralised enforcement in international law, and their risks.
11. Necessity in Art 25 ARSIWA: has the bar been set too high? Use Gabčíkovo, CMS and LG&E.
12. Is international law Eurocentric? Assess Koskenniemi's 'Dealing with Eurocentrism' and its implications for doctrines you studied (statehood, uti possidetis, 'civilized nations').
13. Should remedial secession be recognised as a right under international law? Discuss with reference to Kosovo, Quebec, Badinter and Chagos.
14. Should custom be identified differently depending on the field of international law? Compare North Sea, Nicaragua and Kupreškić.
15. The ICJ (Costa Rica v Nicaragua) and the IACtHR (OC-23/17) both interpret treaties evolutively. Do they do so in the same way, and why? Use Pauwelyn and Elsig.
16. Do reservations to human rights treaties still fit the 1951 consent-based model? Discuss the DRC v Rwanda Joint Separate Opinion and HRC General Comment 24.
