# Week 11: State responsibility II: consequences and invocation

## Rules to know cold

1. Legal consequences (Part Two): the duty of performance continues (Art 29); cessation and, if circumstances require, assurances and guarantees of non-repetition (Art 30); full reparation for injury, material or moral (Art 31, Chorzów).
2. Forms of reparation, in this order: restitution, unless materially impossible or wholly disproportionate (Art 35); compensation for financially assessable damage, including loss of profits (Art 36); satisfaction, such as acknowledgment, regret, apology or a declaration, which must not be humiliating (Art 37). Contributory fault reduces reparation (Art 39).
3. Serious breaches of peremptory norms (Arts 40–41): all States must cooperate to end the breach through lawful means, must not recognise the situation as lawful, and must not render aid or assistance in maintaining it (see the 2004 Wall AO and the 2024 OPT AO).
4. Invocation by an injured State (Art 42): the obligation is owed to it individually; or it is owed to a group or the international community and the State is specially affected, or the obligation is 'integral' (breach affects all parties' performance, e.g. disarmament).
5. Invocation by non-injured States (Art 48): obligations erga omnes partes, protecting a collective interest of a group, or erga omnes, owed to the international community as a whole (Barcelona Traction para 33). They may claim cessation, guarantees, and reparation in the interest of the injured State or beneficiaries (Belgium v Senegal paras 68–70; Climate Change AO 2025 para 443). Gambia v Myanmar, Merits (19 Nov 2026, paras 585–590, 609–610, 621–625) applied this. The Gambia, though not injured, obtained guarantees of non-repetition, restitution and compensation for the Rohingya 'as a group and as individuals' (Myanmar's own population), without needing a mandate from them; it cannot claim reparation for itself. Compensation will be fixed by the ICJ in a later phase.
6. Admissibility (Art 44): nationality of claims rules (diplomatic protection) and exhaustion of local remedies. Loss of the right to invoke through waiver or acquiescence (Art 45).
7. Diplomatic protection (Mavrommatis): by taking up a national's claim, the State asserts its own right to ensure respect for international law in the person of its nationals. It is discretionary for the State.
8. Countermeasures (Arts 49–54) are taken only by an injured State, against the responsible State, to induce compliance. They must be temporary and as far as possible reversible.
9. Countermeasures must be proportionate, commensurate with the injury, taking into account the gravity of the wrong and the rights in question (Art 51; Gabčíkovo para 85; Air Service 'some degree of equivalence').
10. Procedure (Art 52): call on the State to comply, notify the decision and offer to negotiate, though urgent countermeasures are allowed. Suspend them if the wrong has ceased and the dispute is before a tribunal with binding powers.
11. Countermeasures may not affect (Art 50): the prohibition of force, fundamental human rights, humanitarian obligations prohibiting reprisals, jus cogens, or (Art 50(2)) dispute-settlement obligations in force with the target State and the inviolability of diplomatic or consular agents, premises, archives and documents.
12. Third-State countermeasures (Art 54) are left open ('lawful measures'). Contemporary practice, such as sanctions against Russia, adds weight, but they remain contested. Retorsion (unfriendly but lawful acts) is always allowed.
13. Remedies in practice. Restitution was ordered in Tehran Hostages (release, return of premises), Temple of Preah Vihear 1962 (return of objects) and Arrest Warrant 2002 para 76 (cancel the warrant), and refused in Pulp Mills 2010 (dismantling the mill was inappropriate for procedural breaches). Compensation: Corfu Channel (£843,947), Diallo 2012 (US$95,000, of which US$85,000 non-material), Certain Activities 2018 (the ICJ's first award for environmental damage), DRC v Uganda 2022 (US$325m in global sums). Compensation dominates in practice and the injured State may choose it (Art 43(2)(b)). Art 35 restores the situation before the act, not Chorzów's hypothetical situation; compensation covers the gap, such as lost profits. Interest is due where needed for full reparation (Art 38).
14. Jus cogens versus erga omnes: jus cogens concerns the rank of a norm (no derogation, VCLT Art 53); erga omnes concerns to whom the obligation is owed and so who has standing (Barcelona Traction paras 33–34). Every peremptory norm gives rise to erga omnes obligations (ILC 2022, Conclusion 17), but not necessarily the reverse. Arts 40–41 use the first concept, Art 48 the second. There are no 'State crimes': Ago's draft Art 19 (1976) was dropped by Crawford, there are no punitive damages, and obligations under the Genocide Convention 'are not of a criminal nature' (Bosnia Genocide para 170).
15. Standing is not jurisdiction: 'the erga omnes character of a norm and the rule of consent to jurisdiction are two different things' (East Timor 1995 para 29). Art 48 cases still rested on compromissory clauses (CAT Art 30; Genocide Convention Art IX). Several responsible States (Art 47): each may be held responsible, with no double recovery (Corfu Channel: Albania liable although another State probably laid the mines; Nauru 1992: Australia sued alone). The limit is Monetary Gold: no ruling where an absent State's interests form 'the very subject-matter of the decision'. Loss of the right to invoke (Art 45): no fixed time limit, and delay alone does not bar a claim (Nauru para 32).
16. Direct versus indirect injury: the Art 44 admissibility rules (nationality of claims, local remedies) apply only when a State claims for injury to its nationals. Direct injury to the State itself (e.g. an attack on its embassy) needs neither; mixed claims are judged by which element predominates (Avena 2004 para 40: Mexico claimed in its own name, so no exhaustion was required). An Art 48 claim needs no nationality link (Gambia v Myanmar 2022 para 109). The local remedies rule is customary (Interhandel 1959; ELSI 1989) and requires only available and effective remedies.
17. Diplomatic protection, the ILC Articles (2006) in detail: dual nationals may be protected against a third State by either State of nationality (Art 6), but against the other State of nationality only by the State of predominant nationality (Art 7; Mergé 1955; IUSCT Case A/18, 'dominant and effective' nationality). Stateless persons and refugees may be protected by the State of lawful and habitual residence (Art 8, progressive development). Companies are protected by the State of incorporation (Art 9; Barcelona Traction); shareholders only for direct injury to their own rights, or where the company has ceased to exist or had to incorporate in the wrongdoing State (Arts 11–12). 'Protection by substitution' is not custom (Diallo 2007 paras 86–94). Local remedies exceptions (Art 15): no reasonable possibility of effective redress, undue delay, no relevant connection, waiver. There is no individual right to protection in international law (Barcelona Traction paras 78–79), and domestic courts (Abbasi, UK 2002; Kaunda, South Africa 2004; Khadr, Canada 2010) have found none enforceable.
18. Countermeasures by non-injured States (Art 54), the record: the ILC Commentary listed six instances (US against Uganda 1978; Western States against Poland and the USSR 1981; the EC, Australia, Canada and NZ against Argentina 1982; the US against South Africa 1986; against Iraq 1990; the EC against the FRY 1998), found the practice 'sparse' and dropped its 2000 draft allowing collective countermeasures after objections in the Sixth Committee. 'Lawful measures' was chosen so as not to prejudge the answer. Since then the IDI Krakow Resolution (2005, Art 5(c)) accepts non-forcible countermeasures after a widely acknowledged grave breach of an erga omnes obligation, and Tams and Dawidowicz see a permissive rule. Alland's critique: States appoint themselves guardians of the community interest, and only powerful States can do so.

## Key passages

> …reparation must, as far as possible, wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act and re-establish the situation which would, in all probability, have existed if that act had not been committed.
>
> — **Factory at Chorzów (Germany v Poland)**, PCIJ, Series A No 17 (1928), p 47

*Why it matters:* Full reparation (ARSIWA Art 31): restitution first, then compensation, then satisfaction.

> …an essential distinction should be drawn between the obligations of a State towards the international community as a whole, and those arising vis-à-vis another State in the field of diplomatic protection. By their very nature the former are the concern of all States. In view of the importance of the rights involved, all States can be held to have a legal interest in their protection; they are obligations erga omnes.
>
> — **Barcelona Traction (Belgium v Spain)**, ICJ (1970), para 33

*Why it matters:* The basis of ARSIWA Art 48 standing for non-injured States, applied in Gambia v Myanmar.

## What the lecturers stressed

- Art 30 illustrated: cessation (Rainbow Warrior) and guarantees of non-repetition (LaGrand).
- Reparation requires injury plus causation, a 'sufficiently direct and certain causal nexus' (Bosnian Genocide: no compensation for the duty to prevent because it was not shown the genocide would have been averted).
- Art 40: a serious breach is a gross or systematic failure to fulfil a peremptory obligation. Art 41 consequences in practice: the 2024 OPT AO (para 278) — no recognition of changes to the territory's character, composition or status since 1967; abstain from treaty relations concerning the OPT, from economic and trade dealings, and, in establishing and maintaining diplomatic missions in Israel, from any recognition of its illegal presence in the OPT.
- Art 42 injured State: owed individually; or owed to a group or the community and the breach specially affects it or radically changes the position of all others (integral obligations).
- Art 48 non-injured States: obligations erga omnes partes or erga omnes; may claim cessation, guarantees and reparation for the beneficiaries. Quoted: Gambia v Myanmar (Preliminary Objections 2022, para 108) — any State party may invoke responsibility for breaches of the Genocide Convention. Countermeasures to ensure cessation are left to Art 54.
- Admissibility (Art 44): nationality of claims and exhaustion of local remedies, with diplomatic protection as an aside.
- Countermeasures: otherwise wrongful acts, non-forcible (Art 50), aimed at inducing compliance (Art 49), described in the Commentaries as 'a feature of a decentralized system'. They cannot breach peremptory norms, fundamental human rights, humanitarian prohibitions of reprisals, dispute-settlement obligations, or diplomatic inviolability; they must be proportionate (Art 51) and follow procedure (Art 52).
- Gambia v Myanmar, Merits 2026. The judgment cites the 2022 Preliminary Objections judgment at para 109 (common interest of all States parties) and para 112 ('any State party ... may invoke the responsibility of another State party, including through the institution of proceedings before the Court'); the lecture slide quotes the same reasoning from the 2022 judgment as para 108. It adds that Art IX jurisdiction extends to reparation (paras 579-580) and that erga omnes partes standing extends to guarantees of non-repetition and reparation in the interest of the beneficiaries (para 590).

## Cases and materials

### Mavrommatis Palestine Concessions (Greece v UK)
*PCIJ · 1924*

By taking up a national's case, a State asserts its own rights. It also defined a dispute as a disagreement on a point of law or fact, a conflict of legal views or interests.

**Use it for:** Diplomatic protection as the State's own right; the definition of a dispute.

### Air Service Agreement (US/France)
*Arbitral tribunal · 1978*

The US suspension of Air France flights to Los Angeles in response to France's breach of the bilateral agreement was a lawful countermeasure: proportionate, with 'some degree of equivalence', and countermeasures are not barred during negotiations.

**Use it for:** The leading countermeasures case: proportionality and the relation to negotiations.

### Obligation to Prosecute or Extradite (Belgium v Senegal)
*ICJ · 2012*

CAT obligations are erga omnes partes. Every party has a common interest in compliance, so Belgium had standing without being specially affected. Senegal breached Arts 6(2) and 7(1) by failing to prosecute Habré or extradite him.

**Use it for:** Art 48 standing; aut dedere aut judicare.

### Rainbow Warrior (remedies)
*Arbitral tribunal · 1990*

Restitution was impossible because the obligation had expired. The public declaration of breach was appropriate satisfaction, and the tribunal recommended a fund for friendship between the two States.

**Use it for:** Satisfaction; the limits of restitution.

### Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (countermeasures)
*ICJ · 1997*

Variant C (diverting the Danube) was a response to Hungary's wrong but was disproportionate because it deprived Hungary of its right to an equitable share of a shared resource.

**Use it for:** Proportionality; the conditions for countermeasures.

### LaGrand (Germany v US)
*ICJ · 2001*

The US breached the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (Art 36). The Court accepted US assurances as a general guarantee of non-repetition and held that provisional measures are binding.

**Use it for:** Assurances and guarantees of non-repetition (Art 30(b)).

### The Gambia v Myanmar (Merits)
*ICJ · 2026*

A non-injured State party may invoke Myanmar's responsibility and claim cessation, assurances and guarantees of non-repetition, and performance of the obligation of full reparation (restitution and compensation) in the interest of the Rohingya group and individual victims, based on Art 48(2) ARSIWA and Climate Change AO para 443. The State cannot claim reparation for itself (paras 585-590, 609-610).

**Use it for:** Art 48 remedies for erga omnes partes breaches; reparation to individuals or groups via an inter-State case; follows Belgium v Senegal and Preliminary Objections 2022.

### Naulilaa (Portugal v Germany)
*Arbitral tribunal · 1928*

Germany's 1914 attacks on Portuguese forts and posts in Angola, after three Germans were killed at Naulila, were an unlawful reprisal: there was no prior wrongful act (the deaths followed a misunderstanding), no prior demand for redress, and the force was out of all proportion.

**Use it for:** The classic conditions for reprisals (now countermeasures): prior wrong, prior demand, proportionality; armed reprisals are now prohibited (Art 50(1)(a); Res 2625).

### East Timor (Portugal v Australia)
*ICJ · 1995*

Although the right of self-determination is erga omnes, the Court could not rule on Australia's conduct under the Timor Gap Treaty because doing so would require deciding on the lawfulness of Indonesia's conduct, and Indonesia had not consented to jurisdiction (Monetary Gold). 'The erga omnes character of a norm and the rule of consent to jurisdiction are two different things' (para 29).

**Use it for:** Standing under Art 48 does not create jurisdiction; the Monetary Gold limit in cases with several wrongdoers (Art 47).

### Ahmadou Sadio Diallo (Guinea v DRC), Compensation
*ICJ · 2012*

Awarded Guinea US$95,000 for Mr Diallo's unlawful detention and expulsion, including US$85,000 for non-material injury; the sum was intended to repair Mr Diallo's own injury (para 57).

**Use it for:** Moral damage to individuals is compensable; modern diplomatic protection serving human rights.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>Open the questions (answers are folded under each)</summary>

**Q. What are the forms of reparation, in order?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Restitution (Art 35), compensation (Art 36), satisfaction (Art 37), applied singly or combined, with full reparation as the goal (Art 31). Contributory fault reduces it (Art 39).

</details>

**Q. Art 42 versus Art 48: who may invoke, and what can they ask?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Art 42, the injured State: all remedies, plus countermeasures. Art 48, any State for an erga omnes (partes) obligation: cessation, guarantees, and reparation in the interest of the injured or beneficiaries.

</details>

**Q. What are the conditions for lawful countermeasures?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A prior wrongful act by the target; taken by the injured State; aimed at inducing compliance; prior demand and notification with an offer to negotiate (Art 52); proportionate (Art 51); temporary and reversible; not affecting Art 50 obligations; suspended when the matter is before a binding tribunal and the wrong has ceased.

</details>

**Q. Which obligations may never be affected by countermeasures?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Refraining from the threat or use of force; fundamental human rights; humanitarian prohibitions of reprisals; other jus cogens (Art 50(1)); and, for the State taking them, obligations under dispute-settlement procedures in force with the target State and the inviolability of diplomatic or consular agents, premises, archives and documents (Art 50(2)).

</details>

**Q. What are the third-State duties in Art 41 for serious breaches of jus cogens?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Cooperate to end the breach through lawful means; do not recognise the resulting situation as lawful; do not render aid or assistance in maintaining it.

</details>

**Q. What must a claimant satisfy for diplomatic protection?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The nationality of claims: the injured person must be a national (Nottebohm's genuine-link test is contested and is not required by the ILC Articles on Diplomatic Protection 2006, Art 4 and commentary); continuous nationality (Art 5) and exhaustion of effective local remedies (Art 44 ARSIWA; ILC Articles on Diplomatic Protection).

</details>

**Q. What did the 2024 OPT AO (para 278) require of third States?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Not to recognise changes in the physical character, demographic composition, institutional structure or status of the territory occupied since 1967, and to abstain from treaty relations on behalf of the OPT, economic and trade dealings that entrench the occupation, and, in establishing and maintaining diplomatic missions in Israel, from any recognition of its illegal presence in the OPT; States must also take steps to prevent trade or investment relations that help maintain the illegal situation — Art 41 duties applied.

</details>

**Q. Which causation standard governs reparation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A 'sufficiently direct and certain causal nexus' between the wrongful act and the injury (Bosnian Genocide para 462). Serbia's failure to prevent did not lead to compensation because it was not shown the genocide would have been averted; a declaration was satisfaction.

</details>

**Q. Can a non-injured State obtain compensation for victims in an erga omnes partes case?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Yes. In Gambia v Myanmar (Merits, 19 Nov 2026) the ICJ held that The Gambia may claim performance of the obligation of full reparation, including restitution and compensation, in the interest of the Rohingya as a group and as individuals, without a mandate, but not reparation for itself (paras 589-590, 609-610, 624). The amount and modalities will be decided later, after Myanmar reports within two years (para 625).

</details>

**Q. Jus cogens or erga omnes: what is the difference and why does it matter in an answer?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Jus cogens: the norm's rank, no derogation (VCLT 53), triggering Arts 26, 40–41 ARSIWA. Erga omnes: the obligation is owed to all (or to all treaty parties), so any State may invoke it under Art 48 (Barcelona Traction 33–34; Belgium v Senegal). All peremptory norms are erga omnes, not necessarily the reverse. Neither confers jurisdiction (DRC v Rwanda; East Timor para 29).

</details>

**Q. Retorsion, countermeasure, suspension under VCLT Art 60, sanctions: how do they differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Retorsion: lawful in itself, no prior breach needed (expelling diplomats, cutting aid). Countermeasure: otherwise unlawful, wrongfulness precluded (Art 22), answers any breach, temporary, outside Art 50. Art 60: needs a material breach of the same treaty and suspends or ends the treaty itself (Gabčíkovo para 106). Armed reprisals: prohibited. SC sanctions: Chapter VII, need a threat to the peace, not a breach. Interim Accord (2011): Greece pleaded both Art 60 and countermeasures and failed on both.

</details>

**Q. Who bears the risk if a countermeasure rests on a mistaken view that the other State breached first?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The acting State: it judges for itself, and if it is wrong its measure is simply a wrongful act (ILC Commentary to Art 49, para 3). Air Services: countermeasures should be 'a wager on the wisdom, not on the weakness of the other Party'; the right to start them disappears once a tribunal can act (paras 94–96), and the State must expedite the arbitration (para 98).

</details>

**Q. Is moral damage repaired by compensation or by satisfaction?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Non-material harm to individuals (suffering, distress) is compensable (Art 36 Commentary; Diallo 2012: US$85,000). Non-material injury to the State itself (insult to its dignity, a violation of sovereignty without material loss) is repaired by satisfaction (Art 37: acknowledgment, regret, apology, a declaration — Corfu Channel, Rainbow Warrior).

</details>
