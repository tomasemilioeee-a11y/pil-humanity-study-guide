# Week 10: State responsibility I: the wrongful act

## Rules to know cold

1. Art 1 ARSIWA: every internationally wrongful act of a State entails its responsibility. Art 2: an internationally wrongful act is conduct (act or omission) that is (a) attributable to the State and (b) a breach of an international obligation. Damage and fault are not general requirements.
2. Art 4: conduct of any State organ, of any branch or level, is attributable. De facto organs count where there is 'complete dependence' on the State (Bosnia Genocide paras 392–393; Nicaragua para 110).
3. Art 5: persons or entities empowered by law to exercise governmental authority, acting in that capacity. Art 6: organs placed at the disposal of the State by another State.
4. Art 7: ultra vires acts of organs are still attributable if the organ acted in its official capacity.
5. Art 8: private persons acting on the instructions of, or under the direction or control of, the State. The ICJ's test is effective control over the specific operation (Nicaragua para 115; Bosnia Genocide paras 400–407). The ICTY in Tadić (Appeals, paras 115–145) used overall control for organised groups, but the ICJ rejected it for State responsibility (Bosnia para 404), confining it to conflict classification.
6. Art 9: conduct in the absence of official authorities. Art 10: insurrectional movements that become the new government. Art 11: conduct acknowledged and adopted by the State as its own (Tehran Hostages para 74: the approval given by Iran's authorities and their decision to perpetuate the occupation turned the militants' continuing occupation into acts of Iran; mere approval or endorsement is not adoption, per the ILC Commentary to Art 11).
7. Arts 12–15 (breach): breach is determined by the obligation's terms; the obligation must be in force at the time (Art 13); continuing wrongful acts extend over the whole period (Art 14); composite acts (Art 15).
8. Art 16 (aid or assistance), Art 17 (direction and control) and Art 18 (coercion) give derived responsibility in connection with another State's act. Aid requires knowledge, and the act must also be wrongful if committed by the assisting State.
9. Due diligence is a separate route: if conduct isn't attributable, ask whether the State breached its own obligation to prevent, an obligation of conduct measured by its capacity to influence (Bosnia Genocide paras 430–431; Corfu Channel).
10. Circumstances precluding wrongfulness (Arts 20–25): consent, self-defence, countermeasures, force majeure, distress, necessity. None justifies a breach of jus cogens (Art 26). Under Art 27, the duty to comply resumes once the circumstance ends, and compensation for material loss remains possible.
11. Force majeure (Art 23): irresistible force or unforeseen event beyond control making performance materially impossible, not merely more difficult. It is unavailable if the State contributed to it.
12. Distress (Art 24): the author had no other reasonable way to save their own life or the lives of persons in their care.
13. Necessity (Art 25), very strict and cumulative: the only way to safeguard an essential interest against a grave and imminent peril; it does not seriously impair an essential interest of the other State or the international community; the obligation doesn't exclude necessity; and the State has not contributed to the situation (Gabčíkovo paras 51–58).
14. Tehran Hostages shows the two routes to responsibility for private conduct. Phase one, the attack (paras 56–68): the militants were not organs and had not been 'charged by some competent organ ... to carry out a specific operation' (para 58); Khomeini's general anti-American statements were not an authorisation (para 59). So the attack was not attributable, but Iran was responsible for its own omission: it knew of the danger, had the means, and 'failed altogether to take any appropriate steps' to protect the mission (VCDR Art 22(2); paras 61–68). Phase two, the occupation (para 74): approval 'and the decision to perpetuate' the occupation made the militants agents of the State (ARSIWA Art 11). Approval alone is not adoption.
15. Art 7 (ultra vires) in the cases: Caire (France v Mexico, 1929) and Youmans (US v Mexico, 1926): officers or soldiers who use the powers or means of their office engage the State even when they disobey orders. Mallén (1927): an off-duty private assault was not attributable, but a later assault by the same official using his authority was. Modern examples: Velásquez Rodríguez (IACtHR 1988) and the Khashoggi killing (UN Special Rapporteur, 2019). The test is apparent authority (acting 'under colour' of office), not compliance with instructions.
16. Timing of breach: Art 13, the obligation must bind the State when the act occurs (Le Louis, 1817: the slave trade before the prohibition crystallised). Art 14: an expropriation is complete when it happens even if its effects continue, but a continuing denial of access to property is a continuing breach (Loizidou v Turkey, ECtHR). Gabčíkovo para 79: preparatory acts (works that 'could have been abandoned') are not yet a breach; para 80: a duty to mitigate affects the amount of compensation but cannot justify a wrongful act. Rainbow Warrior: the breach continued until the obligation expired on 22 July 1989; responsibility for earlier breaches remained, but there was nothing left to cease.
17. Status of the ARSIWA: GA Res 56/83 (12 Dec 2001) only 'takes note' of them, and a convention has been repeatedly deferred. Courts treat key articles as custom (Gabčíkovo para 52 on necessity; Bosnia Genocide on Arts 4, 8 and 16). Caron's 'paradox of form and authority': because the text looks like a treaty, tribunals may apply it like a statute. Ask article by article whether it codifies custom or develops the law; Arts 41(1), 48(2)(b) and 54 were openly presented as progressive development.
18. Climate Change AO (23 July 2025) on responsibility: breaches of climate obligations are internationally wrongful acts under the general law of State responsibility. A State's failure to take appropriate action to protect the climate system, including through fossil-fuel production, consumption, exploration licences or subsidies, may be an internationally wrongful act attributable to it. Reparation requires a 'sufficiently direct and certain causal nexus'.

## Key passages

> The conduct of a person or group of persons shall be considered an act of a State under international law if the person or group of persons is in fact acting on the instructions of, or under the direction or control of, that State in carrying out the conduct.
>
> — **ILC Articles on State Responsibility (2001)**, Art 8

*Why it matters:* Attribution of private conduct; 'control' read as effective control (Nicaragua, Bosnia).

> For this conduct to give rise to legal responsibility of the United States, it would in principle have to be proved that that State had effective control of the military or paramilitary operations in the course of which the alleged violations were committed.
>
> — **Nicaragua v US**, ICJ Merits (1986), para 115

*Why it matters:* Effective control over the specific operations, not just financing, training and planning.

> …the 'overall control' test has the major drawback of broadening the scope of State responsibility well beyond the fundamental principle governing the law of international responsibility: a State is responsible only for its own conduct…
>
> — **Application of the Genocide Convention (Bosnia and Herzegovina v Serbia and Montenegro)**, ICJ (2007), para 406

*Why it matters:* The ICJ rejects Tadić's overall control for attribution; it may still serve for classifying conflicts.

## What the lecturers stressed

- ARSIWA history: early codification efforts, ILC first reading 1949–1996, second reading 1998–2001, the 2001 Articles 'noted' by the GA (not a treaty, largely custom).
- Structure taught: Part 1 (attribution, breach, CPW), Part 2 (content: reparation, peremptory norms), Part 3 (implementation: invocation, countermeasures).
- Attribution articles on the slides: Art 4 (organs, including police and courts), Art 5, Art 7 (ultra vires), Art 8 (effective control: Nicaragua and Bosnian Genocide; financing and training are not enough; cf. Tadić), Art 10 (insurrection becoming government), Art 11 (acknowledgment). Illustrated with the CIA 'Argo' operation story.
- Breach (Art 12): conduct 'not in conformity with what is required of it by that obligation'; note the question of preparatory acts.
- CPWs are a 'shield rather than a sword', may be justifications or excuses, and leave the underlying obligation intact.
- Argentina cases: the 2001 crisis hit utilities paid in US dollars and indexed to US inflation. BIT Art XI ('measures necessary for the maintenance of public order … essential security interests') versus customary necessity: the CMS annulment committee faulted the tribunal for not considering Art XI on its own terms. ICSID enforcement: consent (Art 25), recognition 'as if it were a final judgment' (Art 54(1)), but no waiver of immunity from execution (Art 55).
- Arts 26–27: no CPW for jus cogens; compliance resumes once the CPW ends, without prejudice to compensation (CMS, LG&E, Gabčíkovo).

## Cases and materials

### Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (Hungary/Slovakia)
*ICJ · 1997*

Hungary's ecological necessity argument failed: the peril was not 'imminent', other means were available, and Hungary contributed. Necessity does not terminate a treaty; it only excuses non-performance while it lasts. Slovakia's Variant C was a disproportionate countermeasure.

**Use it for:** The leading necessity analysis; countermeasure proportionality; necessity versus treaty termination.

### Rainbow Warrior (New Zealand/France)
*Arbitral tribunal · 1990*

France invoked distress and force majeure for removing agents Mafart and Prieur from Hao. The evacuation of Mafart for urgent medical care was justified by distress, but France breached its obligation by not returning him once the danger passed. Prieur's removal (pregnancy, a dying father) was not justified. The declaration of wrongfulness was itself satisfaction.

**Use it for:** Distress versus force majeure; the temporal limit of excuses (Art 27); satisfaction.

### CMS Gas v Argentina
*ICSID · 2005 (award)*

Argentina's 2001–02 crisis measures were not justified by customary necessity: the measures weren't the only way and Argentina contributed. The tribunal read the BIT's Art XI emergency clause through Art 25. The annulment committee (2007) found the tribunal had erred by conflating Art XI with customary necessity, but held this was not an annullable error; it annulled only the umbrella-clause finding, so the damages award stood.

**Use it for:** Strictness of necessity; relation between a treaty clause and custom.

### LG&E v Argentina
*ICSID · 2006*

On a similar crisis, the tribunal accepted that Argentina was in a state of necessity from December 2001 to April 2003, exempting it from compensation for that period under BIT Art XI.

**Use it for:** Shows divergent outcomes; temporal scope of necessity.

### Nicaragua v US (attribution)
*ICJ · 1986*

Even though the US financed, armed, trained and planned for the contras, their acts were not attributable to it without effective control over the specific operations in which the violations occurred (para 115).

**Use it for:** The effective control test (Art 8).

### Prosecutor v Tadić (Appeals)
*ICTY · 1999*

For organised armed groups, 'overall control' (financing, equipping, coordinating or helping plan military activity) suffices to make the group's acts those of the State, internationalising the conflict.

**Use it for:** The rival test, valid for conflict classification only.

### Bosnia v Serbia (Genocide)
*ICJ · 2007*

The Srebrenica genocide was not attributable to Serbia: the VRS was not a de facto organ under complete dependence, and there was no effective control over the operation. Overall control was rejected for attribution. Serbia breached its duty to prevent, given its capacity to influence the Bosnian Serbs, and its duty to punish (failure to cooperate with the ICTY, Mladić).

**Use it for:** Arts 4 and 8; due diligence prevention; tests versus outcomes.

### US Diplomatic and Consular Staff in Tehran (US v Iran)
*ICJ · 1980*

The militants' seizure of the embassy was not attributable to Iran, since they had not been charged with a specific operation by a competent organ; but Iran breached its own duties under the Vienna Conventions by failing to take any appropriate steps to protect the mission. Once Khomeini and other organs approved the occupation and decided to perpetuate it, the militants became agents of Iran (para 74). Diplomatic law is a 'self-contained regime' whose remedies (persona non grata, breaking relations) exclude reprisals against diplomats (paras 83–87).

**Use it for:** Non-attribution does not mean non-responsibility (due diligence); Art 11 adoption requires more than approval; why Art 50(2)(b) protects diplomatic inviolability.

### Caire (France v Mexico)
*French–Mexican Claims Commission · 1929*

Mexico was responsible for the killing of a French national by two officers who acted outside their competence and against orders, because they acted under cover of their official status and used the means placed at their disposal by virtue of that status.

**Use it for:** Art 7 ARSIWA: ultra vires acts in an official capacity are attributable.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>Open the questions (answers are folded under each)</summary>

**Q. What are the two elements of an internationally wrongful act?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Conduct attributable to the State under international law, and a breach of an international obligation of that State (Art 2). Damage and fault aren't required unless the primary rule says so.

</details>

**Q. Effective control versus overall control: which court, and which purpose?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Effective control (ICJ, Nicaragua and Bosnia) is control over the specific operation, used for attribution under Art 8. Overall control (ICTY, Tadić) is general control over the group, used for classifying an armed conflict as international. The ICJ rejected overall control for attribution.

</details>

**Q. When are private militants' acts attributable through Art 11?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

When the State acknowledges and adopts the conduct as its own, clearly and unequivocally, as Iran did in the Tehran Hostages case through official approval plus the decision to perpetuate the occupation (para 74). Approval alone is not enough.

</details>

**Q. What are the cumulative necessity conditions in Art 25?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The only way; an essential interest; grave and imminent peril; no serious impairment of the other State's or the community's essential interest. Barred if the obligation excludes necessity or the State contributed.

</details>

**Q. Distress versus force majeure?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Force majeure: performance is materially impossible because of an irresistible or unforeseen event, so the conduct is involuntary. Distress: there is a choice, but the only reasonable way to save the lives of the agent or persons in their care is to breach.

</details>

**Q. If attribution fails, what is the fallback argument?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Breach of the State's own positive obligation of due diligence (to prevent, protect or punish), measured by knowledge and capacity to influence (Corfu Channel; Bosnia Genocide para 430).

</details>

**Q. What does Art 27 add?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Invoking a circumstance precluding wrongfulness doesn't prejudice compliance once the circumstance ends, nor compensation for material loss.

</details>

**Q. Complicity under Art 16 in Bosnia v Serbia: why wasn't Serbia complicit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Complicity requires at least knowledge of the principal's specific intent (dolus specialis). It was not established that Serbia supplied aid knowing the VRS intended genocide (paras 421–422). Art 16 also requires that the act would be wrongful if committed by the assisting State; the Court applied it by analogy to a non-State principal.

</details>

**Q. Valdoria problem: is the beating of the engineers attributable?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Ironwatch is not an organ (Art 4) and not empowered by law (Art 5). Under Art 8 there were no instructions and no effective control over the beating ('Do what you think best' concerns the convoy). Art 7 ultra vires applies only to organs or Art 5 entities. Art 11 fails: the Minister condemned it, and continued payment is not clear and unequivocal adoption. So it is likely not attributable, but argue Valdoria's own failure of due diligence.

</details>

**Q. Tehran Hostages: how was Iran responsible in each phase?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Phase one: the attack was not attributable (no specific instruction; general encouragement is not enough, paras 58–59), but Iran breached its own duty to protect the mission under VCDR Art 22(2) by doing nothing despite knowledge and means (paras 61–68). Phase two: approval plus the decision to perpetuate the occupation turned the militants into State agents (para 74; Art 11). Approval alone is not adoption.

</details>

**Q. Necessity: which details from Gabčíkovo decide most cases?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The conditions are cumulative and the State 'is not the sole judge' (para 51). 'Imminence' means 'immediacy or proximity', far beyond 'possibility'; a long-term peril counts only if 'certain and inevitable', and scientific uncertainty alone cannot establish a peril (para 54). Costlier alternatives still count as other means (para 55; CMS para 324). A State's own changes of course can amount to contribution (para 57). Wall AO para 140: the wall was not the only means.

</details>

**Q. If fault is not required, why did Corfu Channel turn on Albania's knowledge?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because the primary rule required it: the obligation was not to allow one's territory to be used 'knowingly' for acts contrary to others' rights. ARSIWA is objective (Art 2 Commentary; Bosnia Genocide excluded fault from the general regime). Damage is not required either (Certain Activities/Construction of a Road, 2015). Separate regimes impose strict or absolute liability for harm (1972 Space Liability Convention; Trail Smelter).

</details>

**Q. Justifications versus excuses: why does the label matter for compensation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Consent, self-defence and countermeasures make the act lawful (justifications). Force majeure, distress and necessity excuse conduct that remains wrongful (Paddeu, cited by Hernández), so compensation for material loss may still be owed (Art 27(b); Gabčíkovo para 48; CMS paras 388–394). A treaty emergency clause such as BIT Art XI is a primary rule: if it applies there is no breach and no Art 27(b) question (CMS annulment), which explains why LG&E put losses during the emergency on the investor.

</details>

**Q. When is an ultra vires act of an official attributable?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

When the official acted in an official capacity, using the authority or means of the office, even against orders (Art 7; Caire, Youmans). A purely private act is not attributable (Mallén's off-duty assault), unless the State fails its own due-diligence duty. Art 7 applies only to organs (Art 4) and Art 5 entities, not to private persons under Art 8.

</details>

</details>
