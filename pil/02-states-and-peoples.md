# Week 2: States and peoples

**Mind map:** [States and peoples](https://tomasemilioeee-a11y.github.io/pil-humanity-study-guide/mind-maps/states-peoples.html), interactive, with recall mode and search.

## Rules to know cold

1. Statehood criteria (Montevideo Art 1, reflecting custom): permanent population (no minimum); defined territory (borders need not be settled — North Sea para 46); effective government (strict at birth, loose for existing States; Tinoco: a de facto government binds the State); capacity to enter into relations, i.e. independence (puppet States fail: Manchukuo, TRNC).
2. Additional legality requirement: an entity created through unlawful force or in breach of self-determination or jus cogens is not accepted (SC Res 216/217 Southern Rhodesia, 541 TRNC; Kosovo para 81). Democracy and human rights as emerging criteria (EC Guidelines 1991) remain unsettled.
3. Territory: title by treaty or agreement; uti possidetis juris turns colonial administrative lines into international borders (Burkina Faso/Mali para 23); title prevails over effectivités, which matter only where no title exists. Presumption of continuity: losing an element (e.g. land to sea-level rise) does not necessarily end statehood (Climate AO 2025 para 363).
4. Recognition: declaratory theory dominant (Montevideo Art 3; Badinter Opinion 1) versus constitutive (Oppenheim). Recognition is strong evidence when criteria are doubtful (Kosovo, Palestine); premature recognition can be unlawful intervention; duty of non-recognition for serious breaches of peremptory norms (Namibia, Wall, OPT 2024; ARSIWA Art 41(2)).
5. Treaty-specific (functional) statehood: ICC PTC I (5 Feb 2021) treated Palestine as a 'State' for the Rome Statute after its accession without deciding general statehood. A useful counsel move when general statehood is doubtful.
6. Sovereignty: independence and exclusive State functions over territory (Island of Palmas); sovereign equality (Charter Art 2(1); Res 2625); non-intervention between States, a customary rule (Nicaragua paras 202–205; Res 2625); Art 2(7) itself only bars the UN from intervening in matters of domestic jurisdiction.
7. Self-determination: Charter Arts 1(2), 55; common Art 1 ICCPR/ICESCR; Res 1514 (declaratory of custom by 1965 — Chagos paras 150–153); Res 1541 (independence, free association or integration); Res 2625 (saving clause for States with a government representing the whole people).
8. Who is a 'people'? Clearly colonial peoples, peoples under alien subjugation, domination or exploitation, and under foreign occupation (Kosovo para 79; OPT 2024, where self-determination is peremptory, para 233). Contested: minorities inside existing States.
9. Internal (participation, autonomy) versus external self-determination (independence). External only clearly for colonial and occupied peoples; otherwise it clashes with territorial integrity and uti possidetis. Remedial secession is contested (Quebec Reference obiter; ICJ declined in Kosovo paras 82–83).
10. Kosovo AO (2010): no prohibition of declarations of independence in general IL (para 84); territorial integrity applies between States (para 80); prior illegal declarations were illegal because of force or jus cogens breaches (para 81). It said nothing on statehood or a right to secede.
11. Badinter Commission: Opinion 1 (statehood by usual criteria; recognition declaratory; SFRY in dissolution — dissolution differs from secession, so no parent consent needed and assets and debts are shared); Opinion 2 (self-determination cannot change existing frontiers except by agreement; minorities' rights, arguably overstated as peremptory); Opinion 3 (uti possidetis applied outside the colonial context).
12. Palestine at the ICC: Appeals Chamber 24 Apr 2025 remanded Israel's Art 19(2) challenge (Oslo, statehood) and 15 Dec 2025 (3–2) upheld rejection of Israel's Art 18(1) request. The jurisdiction challenge on remand is pending (no ruling found by Nov 2026), and the warrants stay in force (PTC I, July 2025).
13. Recognition wave (Sept 2025): the UK, Canada, Australia and Portugal (21 Sept) and France, Belgium, Luxembourg, Malta, Monaco and Andorra (22 Sept) recognised Palestine, bringing the total to about 157 of 193 UN members. The GA endorsed the New York Declaration on the two-State solution (142–10–12, 12 Sept 2025). On the declaratory view, recognition is evidence that the criteria are met (or are relaxed for a people with a right to self-determination under occupation, OPT AO 2024 para 233). On the constitutive view, it adds to Palestine's statehood. Not UN membership (SC veto).
14. Chagos follow-up: the UK–Mauritius treaty (signed 22 May 2025: sovereignty to Mauritius, 99-year lease of Diego Garcia) had still not entered into force by Oct 2026. The UK implementing bill stalled after US opposition (Feb 2026), and Mauritius is considering litigation. Teaching point: decolonisation remains incomplete (Chagos AO 2019, Res 1514 para 6) and a signed but unratified treaty does not bind (VCLT Arts 14, 18).
15. Effective government at birth can be relaxed through collective recognition or UN admission: Zaire (1960), Guinea-Bissau, and Croatia and Bosnia-Herzegovina (EC recognition 1992, then UN admission) despite lacking control of much of their territory. Hernández: collective recognition 'cured' the defects, and the two theories coexist. The declaratory theory cannot explain the effect of refusals to recognise; the constitutive theory explains collective recognition but gives a political act legal weight. Lauterpacht's duty to recognise remains a minority view. Contrast the Åland Islands report (1920): Finland was not 'definitely constituted' until its authorities could assert themselves without foreign troops.
16. Recognition of governments is distinct from recognition of States: a coup does not affect the State's identity. De jure recognition needs effective control 'with a reasonable prospect of permanence'; de facto recognition signals doubts about legitimacy (UK–USSR 1921 de facto, 1924 de jure). A government recognised only de facto cannot claim State property or invoke immunity in the recognising State's courts. The US (1977) and UK (1980) stopped formally recognising governments; UK courts apply Somalia v Woodhouse Drake (1993): administrative control, dealings with the UK government, and international recognition in marginal cases. De jure recognition has returned as a policy tool: Libya's NTC (July 2011, before it controlled Tripoli), Venezuela (Guaidó 2019, González 2024), the Taliban (no formal recognition when the book was written, though China accepted a Taliban ambassador in Jan 2024; Russia formally recognised the Taliban government in July 2025, which postdates the book) and Myanmar.

## Key passages

> The state as a person of international law should possess the following qualifications: (a) a permanent population; (b) a defined territory; (c) government; and (d) capacity to enter into relations with other states.
>
> — **Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States (1933)**, Art 1

*Why it matters:* The starting point for any statehood problem; then test effectiveness, legality (Res 2625, 1514) and recognition.

> …the Court considers that general international law contains no applicable prohibition of declarations of independence. Accordingly, it concludes that the declaration of independence of 17 February 2008 did not violate general international law.
>
> — **Accordance with IL of the Unilateral Declaration of Independence in respect of Kosovo**, ICJ Advisory Opinion (2010), para 84

*Why it matters:* A narrow answer: a declaration is not unlawful as such, but the Court said nothing on remedial secession or on whether Kosovo is a State.

## What the lecturers stressed

- Lecture 2 opens with an 'old debate': the Valladolid controversy between Sepúlveda and Las Casas (1550) on the status of indigenous political entities.
- 'Subject of international law' is a descriptive, doctrinal concept, not a legal term of art. Three approaches to identifying subjects, including the purely European perspective (the Bilad Chinguitti in Western Sahara) and the ICJ's functional approach in Reparation.
- Summary slide: States have absolute personality and peoples the right to self-determination; States make international law.
- Identifying subjects, three approaches (slide 7): a purely European perspective (Western Sahara paras 148–152: the Bilad Shinguitti had no personality distinct from its emirates and tribes); the State as the model, looking for State-like entities (Cameroon v Nigeria paras 205–209 on the Kings and Chiefs of Old Calabar); and the possession of rights and obligations, as in Reparation.
- Summary slide 11 in full: States have absolute personality and peoples the right to self-determination; 'surviving historical' subjects are the Holy See and the Sovereign Order of Malta (Spruyt); IOs have functional personality and law-making powers only if their members gave them, and only as far as their functions require; individuals and corporations have limited personality.
- Montevideo beyond Art 1 (slide 14 notes): a federal State is one person (Art 2); political existence is independent of recognition (Art 3); juridical equality (Art 4); recognition is unconditional and irrevocable (Art 6) and may be express or tacit (Art 7); no State may intervene in another's affairs (Art 8). An earlier formula: Deutsche Continental Gas-Gesellschaft v Polish State (1929): a territory, a people and a public power over both.
- Territory covers internal waters, the territorial sea and the airspace above (Nicaragua para 212; Chicago Convention Art 1; UNCLOS). Title passes by treaty or by tacit agreement inferred from conduct, because the law looks to the parties' intentions, not to form (Pedra Branca para 120).
- Uti possidetis in application: rely on the colonial titles as the colonial authorities interpreted and applied them (Benin/Niger 2005 para 140). Title prevails over effectivités, which count only where there is no title (Burkina Faso/Mali para 63, applied to Bakassi in Cameroon v Nigeria).
- Effective government (slide 23): it is needed so that someone protects third States' rights in the territory; a revolutionary government binds the State (Tinoco); and 'failed States' such as Somalia, Afghanistan, Bosnia-Herzegovina and Cambodia keep their statehood despite losing effective government.
- Capacity to enter into relations, 'still relevant?' (slide 24): nineteenth- and early twentieth-century writers (Lawrence 1895; Anzilotti 1929) used it to deny personality to non-European polities. Today it essentially means independence.
- Types of recognition (slide 25): de jure or de facto; premature or late; formal, or informal through admission to IOs, UN membership or direct treaty relations. Constitutive-era examples (slide 27): Island of Palmas (contracts with native princes are not treaties) and Oppenheim (1905: a State becomes an international person 'through recognition only and exclusively').
- Kosovo para 81 lists the declarations the SC condemned: Southern Rhodesia (Res 216 and 217 (1965)), northern Cyprus (Res 541 (1983)) and the Republika Srpska (Res 787 (1992)). They were illegal because of force or jus cogens breaches, not because they were unilateral.
- Sovereign equality (Res 2625, slide 32): juridical equality; the rights inherent in full sovereignty; respect for other States' personality; inviolable territorial integrity and political independence; free choice of political, social, economic and cultural systems; and good-faith compliance. Its manifestations (slide 33): no force, consent as the basis of jurisdiction, non-intervention, equal rights and self-determination, territoriality, and State immunities.
- Self-determination versus the principle of nationalities (slides 36–37): in 1920 the Åland jurists held it was not yet a positive rule; national groups had no right to separate 'by the simple expression of a wish', and disposing of territory was an attribute of every definitively constituted State's sovereignty.
- Res 1514 paras 1–3 (slide 38): alien subjugation denies fundamental human rights; all peoples have the right to self-determination; and 'inadequacy of political, economic, social or educational preparedness should never serve as a pretext for delaying independence'.
- Who is a people (slides 39–40): Kosovo para 79 (a right to independence for non-self-governing territories and peoples under alien subjugation, domination and exploitation); Quebec para 111 (no right of component parts to secede unilaterally); Western Sahara para 59 (the GA may dispense with consulting a population that is not a 'people', or where consultation is unnecessary).
- Attributes of peoples' personality (slides 41–44): the choice of status (Res 1541; Western Sahara para 57); the territorial integrity of a non-self-governing territory, whose status is 'separate and distinct' from the administering State (Res 2625; Chagos; OPT 2024); permanent sovereignty over natural resources (Res 1803 (1962)); wars of national liberation as international armed conflicts (AP I Art 1(4)); and sea-level rise affecting self-determination (Climate AO 2025 para 357).
- Working group readings for Week 2: Chagos paras 25–53 and 139–182; Kosovo paras 51 and 57–122; OPT AO 2024 paras 230–243.

## Cases and materials

### Frontier Dispute (Burkina Faso/Mali)
*ICJ (Chamber) · 1986*

Uti possidetis juris is a general principle aimed at stability: colonial administrative boundaries become international frontiers on independence. Title prevails over effectivités.

**Use it for:** Territory; borders in secession and dissolution.

### Accordance with IL of Kosovo's Declaration of Independence
*ICJ (AO) · 2010*

General IL contains no prohibition of declarations of independence; territorial integrity governs inter-State relations; SC Res 1244 did not bar the declaration, whose authors acted outside the interim framework.

**Use it for:** Secession questions; the limits of the AO.

### Chagos Archipelago AO
*ICJ (AO) · 2019*

Res 1514 reflected custom by 1965; detachment of Chagos without the free and genuine will of the people meant decolonisation was not lawfully completed; the UK must end its administration as rapidly as possible; erga omnes, all States must cooperate.

**Use it for:** Self-determination as custom; GA resolutions as evidence; erga omnes.

### Legal Consequences of Israel's Policies in the OPT AO
*ICJ (AO) · 2024*

Self-determination is peremptory in situations of foreign occupation; Israel's policies amount to annexation; its presence is unlawful; non-recognition and non-assistance duties for all States.

**Use it for:** Peoples under occupation; Art 41 consequences.

### Reference re Secession of Quebec
*Supreme Court of Canada · 1998*

No right to unilateral secession under domestic or international law for a people enjoying internal self-determination; external self-determination arises for colonial or oppressed peoples (remedial secession left open).

**Use it for:** Internal versus external self-determination.

### Tinoco Arbitration (UK v Costa Rica)
*Arbitration (Taft) · 1923*

A de facto government in effective control binds the State, even if unrecognised by others.

**Use it for:** Effective government; recognition's evidentiary role.

### Situation in Palestine (Art 19(3) decision)
*ICC PTC I · 2021*

Palestine is a State Party; for Art 12(2)(a) it is the State on whose territory conduct occurred, covering Gaza and the West Bank including East Jerusalem. Judge Kovács dissented in part (Oslo Accords).

**Use it for:** Treaty-specific statehood.

### Åland Islands (Report of the International Committee of Jurists)
*League of Nations, Committee of Jurists · 1920*

The League was competent over the dispute. Finland, which declared independence in 1917, 'had not yet acquired the status of a definitely constituted State' until a stable political organisation existed and the public authorities could assert themselves throughout the territory without the help of foreign troops. Sovereignty was later confirmed for Finland, with minority guarantees and demilitarisation.

**Use it for:** Effective government as a condition of statehood at birth; self-determination of a minority did not override sovereignty.

### Western Sahara
*ICJ Advisory Opinion · 1975*

Western Sahara was not terra nullius when Spain colonised it. There were legal ties of allegiance between the Sultan of Morocco and some tribes, and some land rights of the Mauritanian entity, but no ties of territorial sovereignty, so nothing displaced Res 1514 and the free and genuine expression of the people's will (para 162). The GA may dispense with consultation where a population is not a 'people' (para 59); Res 1541 offers independence, free association or integration (para 75); the Bilad Shinguitti lacked a personality distinct from its tribes (para 149).

**Use it for:** Self-determination of non-self-governing territories; who is a 'people'; the Eurocentric 'subject' test.

### Land and Maritime Boundary (Cameroon v Nigeria)
*ICJ · 2002*

Sovereignty over the Bakassi Peninsula lay with Cameroon under the 1913 Anglo-German Agreement. The 1884 treaty with the Kings and Chiefs of Old Calabar created a colonial protectorate, not a relationship with a State, so Britain could cede Bakassi (paras 205–209). Nigeria's effectivités could not displace the title (applying Burkina Faso/Mali para 63).

**Use it for:** Title versus effectivités; State-like entities in the colonial period.

### Pedra Branca/Pulau Batu Puteh (Malaysia/Singapore)
*ICJ · 2008*

Sovereignty over Pedra Branca passed to Singapore through the parties' conduct: Johor's 1953 statement disclaiming ownership and Malaysia's silence in the face of Singapore's acts à titre de souverain. Passing of sovereignty may be by treaty or by tacit agreement from conduct; the law emphasises the parties' intentions, not form (para 120). Middle Rocks went to Malaysia.

**Use it for:** Title by tacit agreement and acquiescence.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>Open the questions (answers are folded under each)</summary>

**Q. What are the four Montevideo criteria, and how strict is each?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Permanent population (no minimum); defined territory (borders need not be settled); effective government (strict at birth, loose for existing States); capacity to enter into relations = independence (puppets fail). Plus a legality requirement: no creation by unlawful force or breach of self-determination.

</details>

**Q. Declaratory versus constitutive recognition?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Declaratory (dominant; Montevideo Art 3; Badinter Opinion 1): statehood exists once the criteria are met, recognition acknowledges it. Constitutive (Oppenheim): recognition creates the State. In practice recognition is strong evidence where criteria are doubtful.

</details>

**Q. What is the model-answer chain for a secession question (Azargh)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) Legal basis (common Art 1 Covenants, Res 1514, 1541, 2625); (2) no definition of 'people' — apply the facts; (3) internal versus external; (4) external only for colonial and occupied peoples (Kosovo, Chagos); (5) otherwise uti possidetis and territorial integrity; (6) a declaration itself is not prohibited (Kosovo); (7) statehood outside decolonisation needs the parent State's consent (Badinter, Quebec); (8) remedial secession as a contested extra; (9) conclusion.

</details>

**Q. Dissolution versus secession (Badinter)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Dissolution: the parent State disappears and all units are successors on an equal footing; no parent consent needed; assets, debts and treaties are shared. Secession: the parent continues and its consent matters.

</details>

**Q. What did Chagos say about GA Res 1514 and custom?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Adopted 89–0–9 with no State disputing it, clear normative wording and consistent decolonisation practice, it was declaratory of custom by 1965 (paras 150–153). Territorial integrity of the whole non-self-governing territory is a corollary (para 160).

</details>

**Q. Name the 'treaty-specific statehood' technique and its source.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

ICC PTC I (2021, Palestine): asking whether an entity is a 'State' for the purposes of a particular treaty after its accession, rather than deciding general statehood. Supported by the Heinsch and Pinzauti amicus (effet utile, ASP practice, acquiescence, occupation not defeating statehood).

</details>

**Q. How would a court decide whether an entity is the government of a State, now that the UK no longer recognises governments?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Somalia v Woodhouse Drake (1993): whether it is the constitutional government; the degree and nature of its administrative control over the territory; the UK government's dealings with it; and, in marginal cases, its international recognition. Internationally, de jure recognition requires effective control with a reasonable prospect of permanence and independence from foreign control (contrast Libya's NTC, 2011).

</details>

**Q. What did Western Sahara (1975) decide, and why does it matter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The territory was not terra nullius at colonisation. Ties of allegiance to Morocco's Sultan and some Mauritanian land rights existed, but no ties of territorial sovereignty, so Res 1514 and the people's free and genuine will govern (para 162). Para 59: the GA may dispense with consultation; para 57: the Res 1541 options.

</details>

**Q. How does title relate to effectivités in a territorial dispute?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Where they conflict, the holder of the title prevails; effectivités count where there is no title, or to show how an unclear title was applied (Burkina Faso/Mali para 63 — the slide cites para 68, but the passage is para 63 of the judgment; Cameroon v Nigeria on Bakassi; Benin/Niger para 140). Title can also pass by tacit agreement inferred from conduct (Pedra Branca para 120).

</details>

**Q. What attributes of legal personality do peoples have (Lecture 2)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A right to choose their status (Res 1541; Western Sahara para 57 — the slide cites para 75); the territorial integrity of their territory (Res 2625; Chagos); permanent sovereignty over natural resources (Res 1803); a special status in armed conflict (AP I Art 1(4)); and, per the Climate AO para 357, protection against sea-level rise eroding these rights.

</details>
