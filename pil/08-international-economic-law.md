## Rules to know cold

1. Core GATT disciplines: Art I most-favoured-nation treatment, Art III national treatment for 'like products', Art XI elimination of quantitative restrictions, Art II tariff bindings.
2. Art XX general exceptions use a two-tier test: (1) provisional justification under a paragraph, such as (b) measures necessary to protect human, animal or plant life or health, or (g) measures relating to the conservation of exhaustible natural resources made effective with domestic restrictions; (2) the chapeau: no arbitrary or unjustifiable discrimination where the same conditions prevail, and no disguised restriction on trade (US–Shrimp).
3. Art XXI security exceptions: a measure the member 'considers necessary' for essential security interests, taken in time of war or another emergency in international relations. Russia–Traffic in Transit held it is not fully self-judging. The panel checks objectively that there is an emergency, and the member must articulate its interests in good faith with a minimum plausibility link.
4. Process and production methods: Tuna-Dolphin I (unadopted) refused import bans based on how tuna was caught and on extraterritorial goals. US–Shrimp softened this with a sufficient nexus (migratory turtles) and evolutionary interpretation.
5. Investment law: BITs and the Energy Charter Treaty provide fair and equitable treatment, protection against expropriation without compensation (ECT Art 13), full protection and security, and investor–State arbitration. Withdrawals took effect for the UK (2025) and the EU/Euratom (28 June 2025) after several EU States left, subject to the 20-year sunset clause (Art 47(3)). The modernised ECT applies provisionally from 3 Sept 2025.
6. NIEO Declaration (GA Res 3201 (S-VI), 1974): permanent sovereignty over natural resources, including the right to nationalise, and the Global South's critique of the economic order. The formula that compensation disputes are settled under the nationalising State's domestic law comes from the Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States (GA Res 3281 (XXIX), 1974, Art 2(2)(c)).
7. Factory at Chorzów (Merits, 1928) is the reparation standard: reparation must, as far as possible, wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act. German Interests in Polish Upper Silesia (1926) held that expropriation contrary to the Geneva Convention is unlawful, and that municipal law is a fact before the international court.
8. Links to State responsibility: necessity defences in investment cases (CMS, LG&E) and security exceptions function as treaty-specific circumstances precluding wrongfulness.
9. Current WTO crisis (2025–26): Appellate Body vacant since Dec 2019. The US blocked the 130-member appointment proposal for the 99th time on 24 July 2026 and again on 27 Oct 2026, so panel reports can be appealed 'into the void' (MPIA as workaround). MC14 (Yaoundé, 26–30 Mar 2026) produced no reform declaration, and the e-commerce moratorium lapsed. US tariffs: China's complaints DS633 (Feb 2025) and DS638 ('reciprocal' tariffs, Apr 2025), with the US claiming Art XXI makes them non-justiciable. The US Supreme Court held IEEPA does not authorise tariffs (Learning Resources v Trump, 20 Feb 2026); s.122 tariffs replaced them.
10. Compensation for expropriation, two standards. Capital-exporting States: the Hull formula, 'prompt, adequate and effective' (Hull's note to Mexico, 1938; ECT Art 13). Capital-importing States: 'appropriate compensation' (GA Res 1803 (1962)), settled under the nationalising State's law (CERDS Art 2(2)(c), 1974). Texaco v Libya (1977) treated Res 1803, which Western States had supported, as custom but gave the CERDS clause no opinio juris. Texaco and Aminoil (1982) read 'appropriate' close to Hull, and the 1992 World Bank Guidelines define 'appropriate' as 'adequate, effective and prompt' (constructive ambiguity).
11. Lawful versus unlawful expropriation. A lawful taking must serve a public purpose (read broadly, including police powers, social and economic reform and the environment), be non-discriminatory, not arbitrary or vindictive, follow due process, and be compensated. Compensation is then the investment's value at the moment of dispossession, without future profits (Amoco v Iran, IUSCT 1987). An unlawful taking triggers Chorzów full reparation, including lost profits and possibly a later valuation date (Yukos: valued at the date of the award). Indirect expropriation: interference that makes rights 'so useless that they must be deemed to have been expropriated', even if title remains (Starrett Housing v Iran, IUSCT 1983).
12. BIT protection beyond FET: 'investment' is broadly defined (a contribution, a certain duration, risk, and a contribution to the host State's development), indirect investments through local subsidiaries are covered, and the investment must have been made in accordance with host-State law. FET is tied to the international minimum standard, and full protection and security is a robust duty that applies even in armed conflict. National treatment and MFN apply (MFN can import better standards from other BITs; ECT Art 16). ISDS bypasses local remedies, unlike diplomatic protection, and once the host State consents to ICSID the investor's home State may not exercise diplomatic protection (ICSID Art 27). There is no appeal, only annulment (Art 52). Denunciations: Bolivia 2007, Ecuador 2009 (rejoined 2021), Venezuela 2012, Honduras 2024.
13. WTO enforcement is a special countermeasures regime. If the losing Member does not comply within the reasonable period, the DSB may authorise the winner to suspend concessions ('retaliation', DSU Art 22): first in the same sector, then other sectors or agreements (Art 22(3)), at a level equivalent to the nullification or impairment (arbitration under Art 22(6)–(7)). EC–Hormones is the leading example. Because DSU Art 23 bars unilateral determinations, general-law countermeasures for WTO breaches are excluded: a lex specialis under ARSIWA Art 55.
14. Art XXI and the ICJ: in Nicaragua (1986, para 222) the ICJ held that the US–Nicaragua FCN Treaty's security clause was not self-judging precisely because, unlike GATT Art XXI, it lacked the words 'which it considers'. Russia–Traffic in Transit later read those words as making only 'necessary' self-judging. Follow-ups: in Saudi Arabia–IPRs (2020) one measure (not acting against the pirate broadcaster beoutQ) failed the plausibility test; in Dec 2022 panels found US steel and aluminium tariffs and the 'made in China' marking rule for Hong Kong goods were not 'taken in time of' an emergency (the US appealed into the void).
15. Yukos and treaty law: Russia signed but never ratified the ECT. The tribunal read Art 45(1)'s proviso ('to the extent ... not inconsistent with its constitution, laws or regulations') as asking whether provisional application as such was consistent with Russian law, so the arbitration clause applied (cf VCLT Art 25). The taxation carve-out (Art 21) covers only bona fide taxation. The Hague District Court set the awards aside (2016); the Court of Appeal reinstated them (2020); the Dutch Supreme Court dismissed Russia's final appeal on 17 Oct 2025. Russia–Transit and Yukos both 'look behind the label' (security, taxation).
16. Hernández's framing: international economic law is 'not a neutral framework' but an outgrowth of colonial economic relations. The NIEO was not radical (it used the language of sovereignty and consent) and ended as a 'relative failure'. The neoliberal Bretton Woods consensus has 'survived almost wholly intact', its institutions are dominated by powerful States, and IMF/World Bank loan conditionality imposes structural adjustment; the AIIB and the BRICS New Development Bank are alternatives. Developing States gained only GATT Part IV (1965, non-reciprocity) and the 1979 Enabling Clause (optional 'differential and more favourable treatment').

## What the lecturers stressed

- IEL under pressure: backlash against globalisation, trade wars since 2016, supply-chain vulnerability and 'economic security' (energy, raw materials, semiconductors), an emerging multipolar order, a paralysed WTO (Appellate Body crisis since 2019), and contested investor–State arbitration.
- Origins: Bretton Woods 1944 (World Bank, IMF), GATT 1947, BITs from 1959, the NIEO 1974 (participation in lawmaking, permanent sovereignty over natural resources, regulating TNCs, fair commodity prices, technology transfer), WTO 1995 (166 members).
- Rationales: Ricardo's comparative advantage ('size of the pie'), terms-of-trade theory (reciprocal binding avoids an inefficient Nash equilibrium; Bagwell and Staiger), credibility towards domestic lobbies, and preventing trade wars (Smoot-Hawley 1930). Distribution matters: the Lakner–Milanovic 'elephant graph'.
- WTO dispute settlement is compulsory (Art 6.1 DSU, negative consensus), exclusive (Art 23.1 DSU: no unilateral determinations, no other court decides WTO breaches) and contentious (no advisory opinions).
- Like products (Philippines – Distilled Spirits): physical characteristics, end use, tariff classification, consumer tastes and preferences. Non-product-related PPMs are the hard case.
- Art XX two-tier test (US – Gasoline, US – Shrimp): first a paragraph, then the chapeau, cumulatively. Under (b) the Member chooses its level of protection (Brazil – Retreaded Tyres) but must use the least trade-restrictive reasonably available measure (Thailand – Cigarettes); once necessity is shown, the complainant must prove an alternative (US – Gambling). Under (g) 'exhaustible natural resources' is read widely (clean air in US – Gasoline; living turtles in US – Shrimp) and 'relating to' needs only a rational connection, so environmental cases go under (g).
- Art XXI has no chapeau. It lay dormant 1947–2016: Sweden's 1975 footwear restrictions on 'security' grounds (withdrawn), and the 1985 US embargo on Nicaragua, where the panel was barred from judging the invocation.
- Investment law: protection against direct or indirect expropriation without compensation, a public-purpose requirement (German Interests in Polish Upper Silesia: 'public utility, judicial liquidation and similar measures'), national and MFN treatment. Origins in protecting Global North assets after decolonisation (Suez 1956).

## Cases and materials

### US — Restrictions on Imports of Tuna (Tuna-Dolphin I)
*GATT panel (unadopted) · 1991*

A US import ban on tuna caught with dolphin-unsafe methods violated Art XI and could not be justified under Art XX(b) or (g), which do not protect resources outside the importing State's jurisdiction. Measures cannot regulate production methods abroad.

**Use it for:** The traditional view against extraterritorial and production-method measures.

### US — Shrimp (AB)
*WTO Appellate Body · 1998*

Sea turtles are 'exhaustible natural resources' (evolutionary interpretation using UNCLOS and CITES). The measure related to conservation under Art XX(g), but failed the chapeau because it was applied rigidly, without negotiation, in an arbitrary and unjustifiably discriminatory way.

**Use it for:** The two-tier Art XX test; environmental measures; the chapeau.

### Russia — Traffic in Transit
*WTO panel · 2019*

Panels have jurisdiction over Art XXI. The existence of an 'emergency in international relations' (here Russia–Ukraine 2014) is objectively determinable. Members define their own essential security interests in good faith, but measures must meet a minimum plausibility requirement. Russia's measures were justified.

**Use it for:** Security exceptions are justiciable; the scope of self-judging clauses.

### Yukos Universal v Russia
*PCA (ECT tribunal) · 2014*

Russia's tax measures against Yukos amounted to an indirect expropriation under ECT Art 13. Across the three parallel Yukos shareholder awards (Hulley, Yukos Universal, Veteran), damages assessed at about USD 66.7bn were reduced by 25% for contributory fault, to about USD 50bn in total (Yukos Universal's own share was about USD 1.8bn).

**Use it for:** Investment protection; expropriation; contributory fault (Art 39 ARSIWA analogy).

### German Interests in Polish Upper Silesia / Chorzów Factory
*PCIJ · 1926 / 1928*

Poland's expropriation of German-owned property breached the Geneva Convention. The 1928 merits judgment set the full-reparation standard: restitution in kind or its monetary equivalent, so as to wipe out the consequences.

**Use it for:** Unlawful expropriation; the reparation standard (Art 31 ARSIWA).

### US — Gasoline
*WTO Appellate Body · 1996*

Established the two-tier analysis of Art XX. Clean air as an exhaustible natural resource was the panel's finding, which was not appealed. The measure was justified under (g) but failed the chapeau.

**Use it for:** Order of analysis; wide reading of (g).

### Brazil — Retreaded Tyres
*WTO Appellate Body · 2007*

Members may set their own level of health and environmental protection; necessity is a weighing exercise and an import ban can be necessary if it contributes materially to the aim.

**Use it for:** Art XX(b) necessity.

### Philippines — Distilled Spirits
*WTO Appellate Body · 2012*

Likeness under Art III judged by physical characteristics, end use, tariff classification and consumer preferences, with competition between the products at the core.

**Use it for:** National treatment, 'like products'.

### Texaco Overseas Petroleum v Libya
*Sole arbitrator (Dupuy) · 1977*

Libya's nationalisation of oil concessions breached the concession contracts. GA Res 1803 (1962), adopted with broad support including capital-exporting States, reflected custom on permanent sovereignty and 'appropriate compensation'; the compensation clause of the Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States (Art 2(2)(c)) lacked opinio juris because the main capital-exporting States opposed it.

**Use it for:** GA resolutions as evidence of custom (links to week 5); the compensation debate (Hull v 'appropriate').

### Amoco International Finance Corp v Iran
*Iran–US Claims Tribunal · 1987*

Distinguished lawful from unlawful expropriation: for a lawful taking, compensation is the value of the undertaking at the date of dispossession, without future lost profits; only an unlawful taking calls for full Chorzów reparation including lucrum cessans.

**Use it for:** Quantum depends on whether the taking was lawful (public purpose, non-discrimination, due process, compensation).

### EC — Measures Concerning Meat and Meat Products (Hormones)
*WTO Appellate Body · 1998*

The EC's ban on hormone-treated beef breached the SPS Agreement because it was not based on a proper risk assessment (Art 5) or international standards (Art 3). When the EC failed to comply, the US and Canada were authorised in 1999 to suspend concessions equivalent to their losses (DSU Art 22.7); the dispute ended in a mutually agreed solution.

**Use it for:** WTO retaliation as an institutionalised countermeasure; science-based limits on health measures.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>Open the questions (answers are folded under each)</summary>

**Q. What is the two-tier test under GATT Art XX?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) The measure falls within a paragraph, e.g. (b) is 'necessary' to protect health (weighing and balancing, less trade-restrictive alternatives), or (g) 'relates to' conservation and is even-handed. (2) It meets the chapeau: no arbitrary or unjustifiable discrimination and no disguised restriction.

</details>

**Q. Is GATT Art XXI self-judging after Russia–Traffic in Transit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Partly. 'Which it considers' applies to necessity and to defining security interests (subject to good faith and plausibility). Whether there is war or an emergency in international relations is objective and reviewable by the panel.

</details>

**Q. Why does US–Shrimp matter beyond trade law?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It uses systemic and evolutionary interpretation (VCLT Art 31(3)(c)), integrating environmental treaties into WTO law and accepting measures with a nexus to a resource beyond the regulating State's territory.

</details>

**Q. What is the Chorzów Factory formula?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Reparation must, as far as possible, wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act and re-establish the situation that would in all probability have existed had the act not been committed.

</details>

**Q. Why is WTO dispute settlement called compulsory, exclusive and contentious?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Compulsory: a panel is established unless the DSB decides by consensus not to (Art 6.1 DSU). Exclusive: Members must use the DSU to redress WTO breaches and may not decide unilaterally (Art 23.1 DSU). Contentious: no advisory opinions, only rulings in disputes.

</details>

**Q. Why invoke Art XX(g) instead of (b)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(g) only requires the measure to 'relate to' conservation (a rational connection) and to be even-handed with domestic restrictions, which is more deferential than (b)'s 'necessary' (least trade-restrictive alternative). Exhaustible resources are read widely, so environmental measures usually go under (g).

</details>

**Q. EU raw-materials mock: China's claims and the EU's defences?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Claims: lower tariffs for 'friendly nations' breach MFN (Art I:1) and bindings (Art II); quotas on Chinese imports breach Art XI (and possibly XIII). Defences: Art XXI(b)(iii) emergency in international relations (objectively reviewable after Russia – Traffic in Transit; plausibility and good faith), Art XX(j) short supply or (g) only with difficulty; any regional-integration exception (Art XXIV) does not cover a political 'friends' list.

</details>

**Q. Can the US rely on Art XXI GATT to shield its 2025 tariffs from WTO review?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The US says Art XXI is self-judging and non-justiciable (DS633, DS638). Russia–Traffic in Transit (2019) holds that panels review whether an 'emergency in international relations' objectively exists and whether measures meet a minimum plausibility and good-faith test. Generalised 'reciprocal' tariffs on all trading partners are hard to tie to an emergency. Even a winning complainant faces an appeal into the void because the AB is non-functional (99th US block, July 2026).

</details>

**Q. How does compensation differ for lawful and unlawful expropriation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Lawful (public purpose, non-discriminatory, due process, compensation offered): the value of the investment at the time of the taking, usually without future profits (Amoco v Iran). Unlawful: full reparation under Chorzów, which can include lost profits and a later valuation date (Yukos valued at the award date). The standard itself is disputed: Hull ('prompt, adequate and effective') versus 'appropriate' (Res 1803; Texaco).

</details>

**Q. What does Nicaragua (1986) para 222 add to the debate on GATT Art XXI?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The ICJ held the US–Nicaragua treaty's security clause was not self-judging because it lacked the words 'which it considers', which GATT Art XXI contains. So the wording matters: Russia–Transit treats 'which it considers' as making only necessity self-judging, while the existence of an emergency stays objective. Compare the ICJ's good-faith review of a self-judging clause in Djibouti v France (2008, para 145).

</details>

**Q. How is WTO retaliation different from a countermeasure under general international law?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It needs a prior panel/AB ruling and DSB authorisation after non-compliance; it follows a sector sequence (DSU Art 22(3)); its level must be equivalent to the nullification or impairment, checked by arbitration; and unilateral countermeasures for WTO breaches are barred (DSU Art 23). General countermeasures (ARSIWA Arts 49–53) are self-judged and need only a prior wrong, a demand and proportionality.

</details>
