# Lectures 3–4: Actors, obligations and peacetime frameworks

## Rules to know cold

1. There is a tripartite typology of State obligations: respect (negative), protect (positive, against third parties: due diligence, Osman test of knew or ought to have known of a real and immediate risk), and fulfil (provide).
2. Human rights obligations have an objective, non-reciprocal character (Mégret). Erga omnes partes in treaties; interstate complaints (ECHR Art 33; CAT Art 21).
3. IHL binds all parties to an armed conflict, including non-state armed groups (CA3; APII). Common Article 1 (respect and ensure respect) creates internal and external dimensions.
4. ICL obligations between States: aut dedere aut judicare (CAT Art 7, the Grave Breaches regime), universal jurisdiction, and cooperation with courts (Rome Part 9).
5. Non-state actors (Clapham): armed groups bound by IHL (and arguably IHRL where they control territory). Corporations have no direct treaty obligations: UNGPs (protect, respect, remedy), domestic civil liability (Vedanta, Okpabi), and the draft business and human rights treaty.
6. Individuals are duty bearers under ICL, while under IHRL the State is the duty bearer.
7. Limitations: an interference is lawful if (1) prescribed by law, (2) pursues a legitimate aim, and (3) is necessary in a democratic society (pressing social need, proportionality, margin of appreciation). Abuse of limitations to silence opposition is caught by Art 18 ECHR (Navalny v Russia; Merabishvili).
8. Derogation (ICCPR Art 4; ECHR Art 15): a public emergency threatening the life of the nation, officially proclaimed and notified; measures strictly required by the exigencies; consistent with other IL obligations; non-discriminatory. Non-derogable rights include life (except lawful acts of war under ECHR Art 15(2)), torture, slavery, non-retroactivity, plus the core of fair trial (HRC GC 29).
9. Absolute rights: the prohibition of torture and ill-treatment can't be limited or derogated from (Mavronicola: absoluteness means no justification, even when the threat is grave). Link to jus cogens.
10. The normalisation of emergency in counter-terrorism (SR report A/HRC/37/52): permanent emergencies, vague definitions, abuse against civil society.
11. Mégret frames the 'special character' of human rights obligations as a clash of form and function, quoting Craven: the treaty form suggests rights-holders are merely 'fortuitous beneficiaries' of a contract between States, while the regime's purpose is quasi-constitutional. Taken to its extreme, ratification is 'declaratory of states' obligations rather than constitutive of them', and human rights obligations become the typical erga omnes obligations, justifying routine scrutiny of other States without any injury of one's own.
12. Earliest authorities for the special character of human rights treaties: ICJ, Reservations to the Genocide Convention AO (1951) para 23: the parties 'do not have any interests of their own', only a common interest, so there is no 'perfect contractual balance'; and a State can become a party despite objections to its reservation (para 24). European Commission, Austria v Italy (Pfunders, 1961): ECHR obligations are 'essentially of an objective character'. Consequence for invalid reservations: severability, so the State stays bound without the reservation (Loizidou v Turkey; Belilos).
13. Withdrawal from human rights treaties. Where there is a denunciation clause (ECHR Art 58, ACHR Art 78, CERD Art 21), it has rarely been used: Greece left the ECHR 1969–74 after the coup, Trinidad and Tobago denounced the ICCPR Optional Protocol and the ACHR (1998–99), Venezuela the ACHR (2013), and none had retroactive effect. Where there is none (ICCPR), HRC General Comment 26 (after North Korea's 1997 attempt) says the Covenant implies no right of denunciation and its protection 'devolves with territory'. Mégret calls this the unique 'stickiness' of human rights commitments, in contrast with Rome Statute Art 127.
14. Clapham identifies five pressures that make non-state actors a human rights problem: globalisation, privatisation, fragmentation, feminisation and criminalisation. Privatisation 'relabels' the same act: what was a human rights violation becomes a tort or breach of contract, and the private actor can invoke competing rights of its own. Criminalisation shows international law already binds non-state individuals, and he sees 'no convincing conceptual reason' why it should not extend to corporations and rebel groups as such.
15. Clapham on armed groups: the ICRC position is that non-state armed groups generally have no IHRL obligations, except where they exercise de facto State-like authority, and even then speaks only of 'responsibilities' (Breitegger). Clapham replies that each of Sivakumaran's seven routes explaining why armed groups are bound by IHL (ratification by the State, domestic law, treaties binding third parties, consent to CA3, claims to represent the State, exercising State-like functions, custom) could equally ground IHRL obligations.
16. Counter-view (Bantekas and Oette): extending human rights obligations to outlawed non-state actors such as terrorist groups risks making States 'irresponsible', because it shrinks the State's positive duty to prevent and punish into a merely negative one. Criminal law handles such conduct better. They accept complementary obligations for corporations, and conclude that armed groups uphold rights only through their own internal processes, a 'cherry-picking' that breeds uncertainty and lets States blur their own commitments. Alston (on the Tamil Tigers): no ICCPR obligations, but the UDHR's 'common standard of achievement' applies to any group that aspires to represent a people.
17. Rebel groups and human rights (Bantekas and Oette): CA3, APII and API Art 1(4) address armed groups directly, and ARSIWA Art 10(2) (the conduct of a successful insurrectional movement is attributed to the new State) can be read as giving rebels obligations even before they take power. Rebel human rights obligations are strongest where they control territory, since the territorial State cannot protect people there, and are reinforced by SC resolutions addressed to insurgents. Below the armed-conflict threshold rebels are treated like terrorist groups, without human rights obligations. Illustration: the CPP-NPA's internal purges in the Philippines (Oplan Missing Link, 1988), where victims of torture by their own comrades found no forum.

## What the lecturers stressed

- Tension: IHRL is a branch of PIL, built on sovereignty and consent, yet it constrains sovereignty. Human rights treaties are 'special': States bind themselves to other States but create rights for individuals (Mégret).
- IACtHR Advisory Opinion OC-2/82 (Effect of Reservations): human rights treaties 'are not multilateral treaties of the traditional type … for the reciprocal exchange of rights'; States assume obligations 'towards all individuals within their jurisdiction'. Loizidou: the ECHR is an 'instrument of European public order'. Erga omnes obligations originate in Barcelona Traction.
- Respect, protect, fulfil. Protect means proactively ensuring persons do not suffer violations, a due diligence duty prominent in violence against women (Velásquez Rodríguez v Honduras). 'Doing nothing is not an option.'
- IHL: who is bound, and do its rules create individual rights? Context and status are key (hostilities or occupation; civilian, combatant or POW).
- Common Article 1 with Pictet's gloss: respect for the Conventions is asserted 'as much because of the respect one has for it oneself as because of the respect one expects from one's opponent'. Nicaragua v Germany (ICJ) as the live example.
- Grave breaches enforcement: GCI Art 49 (GCII 50, GCIII 129, GCIV 146, API): enact penal sanctions, search for alleged offenders 'regardless of their nationality', try or hand over, with fair-trial safeguards.
- ICL: what is its purpose — justice, accountability, punishment? Supranationality, aut dedere aut judicare, cooperation and arrest warrants, States parties versus non-parties, groups.
- Lecture 4 (Peace): interdependence and indivisibility. Vasak's 'generations' are no longer a useful construct; indivisibility is accepted in principle but delivery differs. Two-track realisation: essential and immediate versus progressive. Emergence of solidarity rights. Absolute versus non-absolute rights, shaped by jus cogens.
- Reservations: VCLT Art 2 definition; must not defeat object and purpose (Art 19(3)). Given indivisibility, many human rights reservations are questionable. HRC General Comment 24: no reservations to core guarantees such as the right to a remedy; reservations must be specific, transparent, refer to a specific provision, and not be general. Example: Qatar's reservation to ICCPR Art 3 and the objections to it.
- Limitations define a right's scope; they are built into specific clauses (ICCPR Arts 18(3), 19(3)). Margin of appreciation adds a pluralist element but defers to States, controversially where there is no global consensus.
- Five headings for assessing a limitation: prescribed by law (accessible, foreseeable, precise — Republican Party of Russia v Russia 2011); legitimate aim (exhaustive and narrowly construed list); necessary in a democratic society (pluralism, tolerance, broadmindedness — democracy is not majority rule); pressing social need; proportionality (if a less severe measure would do, the restriction fails).
- Abuse of limitations: Navalnyy v Russia (GC 2018) under Art 18 ECHR. Merabishvili v Georgia: restrictions can pursue a plurality of purposes; Art 18 is violated where the ulterior purpose is predominant.

## Cases and materials

### Osman v UK
*ECtHR · 1998*

Under Art 2 a State has a positive obligation to take reasonable operational measures where authorities knew or ought to have known of a real and immediate risk to an identified individual's life from third parties.

**Use it for:** The duty to protect, a due diligence standard.

### Lawless v Ireland / A and Others v UK
*ECtHR · 1961 / 2009*

An emergency threatening the life of the nation is a wide margin for the State. In A v UK, indefinite detention of foreign terror suspects only was disproportionate and discriminatory, despite a valid derogation.

**Use it for:** Review of derogation measures; non-discrimination.

### Navalny v Russia (GC)
*ECtHR · 2018*

Repeated arrests at protests pursued the ulterior purpose of suppressing political pluralism, a violation of Art 18 together with Arts 5 and 11.

**Use it for:** Abuse of permissible limitations.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>Open the questions (answers are folded under each)</summary>

**Q. What is the three-step test for limiting a qualified right?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Prescribed by law (accessible and foreseeable), a legitimate aim, and necessary in a democratic society (proportionate, least restrictive), within the margin of appreciation.

</details>

**Q. What are the conditions for a valid derogation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A public emergency threatening the life of the nation; official proclamation and notification; measures strictly required by the exigencies; consistent with other IL obligations; non-discriminatory; non-derogable rights are untouched.

</details>

**Q. What does Common Article 1 require?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

To respect and ensure respect for the Conventions in all circumstances: internally (one's own forces and population) and externally (not encouraging, aiding or assisting violations by others, and taking feasible steps to bring them to an end, per the ICRC 2016 Commentary).

</details>

**Q. How are corporations bound in the protection of humanity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Indirectly: States' duty to protect (CESCR GC 24), UNGP soft law (the responsibility to respect, human rights due diligence), the OECD Guidelines, domestic tort litigation, and ICL for individuals within companies (Lundin trial).

</details>

**Q. Why are human rights treaties described as 'special' (OC-2/82)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

They are not reciprocal exchanges of rights between States; their object is protecting individuals irrespective of nationality, so States assume obligations towards all individuals within their jurisdiction. This explains, e.g., the treatment of reservations and erga omnes partes standing.

</details>

**Q. What does HRC General Comment 24 say about reservations?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Reservations incompatible with object and purpose are invalid; core guarantees (e.g. the right to a remedy, peremptory norms) cannot be reserved; reservations must be specific and transparent and refer to particular provisions. The HRC claims competence to assess validity and to sever invalid reservations.

</details>

**Q. Merabishvili's contribution to Art 18 ECHR?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A restriction may pursue several purposes, some legitimate and some not. Art 18 is violated where the illegitimate (ulterior) purpose was predominant, assessed on all the circumstances.

</details>

**Q. Clapham versus Bantekas and Oette: should armed groups have human rights obligations?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Clapham: yes. The routes that bind armed groups to IHL (Sivakumaran's seven) work equally for IHRL; UN COIs, SC resolutions and truth commissions already demand it; we must question who the duty-bearers are (ch 28, pp 571–578). Bantekas and Oette: be careful. Making outlawed groups duty-bearers lets States off their positive duties; criminal law is the better tool, though rebels controlling territory should answer for fundamental rights (ch 19, pp 1430, 1467).

</details>

**Q. Can a State withdraw from the ICCPR?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No, according to HRC General Comment 26 (1997, after North Korea tried): the Covenant has no denunciation clause and is not the type of treaty that implies one; its rights 'belong to the people' and devolve with territory, whatever changes in government. The UN Secretary-General as depositary agreed. Mégret calls this 'stickiness'; contrast ECHR Art 58 and Rome Statute Art 127.

</details>

**Q. What are Clapham's five pressures, and what does 'relabelling' mean?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Globalisation, privatisation, fragmentation (armed groups, international organisations), feminisation (private violence against women) and criminalisation. Relabelling: once a public function is privatised, the same harm is treated as a tort or contract breach, not a human rights violation, and the private actor can raise its own rights (e.g. property in a privatised shopping centre, Appleby v UK).

</details>
