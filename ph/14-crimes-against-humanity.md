# Lecture 18: Crimes against humanity

**Mind map:** [Crimes against humanity](https://tomasemilioeee-a11y.github.io/pil-humanity-study-guide/mind-maps/crimes-against-humanity.html), interactive, with recall mode and search.

## Rules to know cold

1. Rome Art 7(1) chapeau: any listed act committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack directed against any civilian population, with knowledge of the attack.
2. 'Attack' (Art 7(2)(a)): a course of conduct involving the multiple commission of listed acts against a civilian population, pursuant to or in furtherance of a State or organisational policy. It need not be a military attack. No armed conflict nexus is required (Tadić; CCL 10).
3. Widespread means large-scale (number of victims or geographic scope). Systematic means organised nature, a pattern, non-accidental repetition (Kunarac). The test is disjunctive.
4. Civilian population: the population must be the primary object of the attack, predominantly civilian (the presence of some combatants doesn't change this). 'Any': nationality is irrelevant, including a State's own nationals.
5. Policy element: active promotion or encouragement by a State or organisation. The organisational policy debate (Kenya decision: the majority applied a capacity-based test, while Judge Kaul's dissent required State-like organisations). The ICTY didn't require policy (Kunarac).
6. Mental element: knowledge of the attack and that the conduct is part of it (not of all details). Motive is irrelevant.
7. Listed acts: murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation or forcible transfer, imprisonment, torture, sexual violence (rape, sexual slavery, enforced prostitution, forced pregnancy, enforced sterilisation), persecution, enforced disappearance, apartheid, and other inhumane acts.
8. Persecution (7(1)(h); 7(2)(g)): an intentional and severe deprivation of fundamental rights, contrary to IL, by reason of the identity of a group (political, racial, national, ethnic, cultural, religious, gender, or other universally recognised grounds), in connection with another Art 7 act or crime under the Court's jurisdiction. Gender persecution is advancing: PTC II warrants of 8 July 2025 against Taliban leader Akhundzada and Chief Justice Haqqani (the first ICC gender-persecution warrants; 'gender' goes beyond biological sex; two more warrants Sept 2025, all unsealed 5 Oct 2026), while Al Hassan (2024) shows its limits: he was convicted of persecution on religious grounds, but the Chamber split on gender persecution, so there was no gender-persecution conviction. The concept of gender is debated (Art 7(3) definition dropped in the draft CAH convention).
9. Other inhumane acts (7(1)(k)): of a similar character, causing great suffering or serious injury, a residual category catching new harms. Forced marriage (AFRC Appeals SCSL; Ongwen ICC 2021).
10. Apartheid (7(2)(h)): inhumane acts in the context of an institutionalised regime of systematic oppression and domination by one racial group with the intention of maintaining that regime. Enforced disappearance (7(2)(i)).
11. Draft Articles on CAH (ILC 2019): GA Res 79/122 (4 Dec 2024) set the path to a convention: Preparatory Committee session 1 held 19–30 Jan 2026, session 2 on 12–15 Apr 2027, and a diplomatic conference in 2028 and 2029. The convention would cover prevention, punishment, and aut dedere aut judicare. This fills the gap, since there is no CAH treaty, unlike genocide and war crimes (Sadat).
12. Gender persecution update: the ICC unsealed (5 Oct 2026) two further Taliban warrants (Ministers Habibullah Agha and Neda Mohammad Nadim) alongside the July 2025 warrants for Akhundzada and Haqqani. PTC II held that 'gender' in Art 7(3) requires an interpretation beyond a purely biological conception of sex, and that gender persecution covers systemic, institutionalised harm such as discriminatory societal norms.
13. Enforced disappearance: Rome Art 7(2)(i) is narrower than the 2006 Convention (ICPPED Art 2). Rome adds an intent to remove the person from the protection of the law, 'for a prolonged period', and requires a State or 'political organization' plus a widespread or systematic attack. Under ICPPED a single case is a violation and only a widespread or systematic practice is a CAH (Art 5). Exam hook: is a cartel that counts as an 'organization' for the Art 7(2)(a) policy also a 'political organization' for Art 7(2)(i)? Burundi (PTC III, 2017) inferred the intent from the arbitrary manner of arrests.
14. Kress argues the structural difference between genocide and CAH is 'essentially technical': both are systemic crimes. In CAH the collective activity (the widespread or systematic attack) is an objective contextual element; in genocide it is the objective point of reference of the required intent. So in both, an initial act in an emerging campaign can be a completed crime (the Elements on 'initial acts in an emerging pattern' and on furthering an 'emerging' attack). Both definitions are drafted from the perspective of those who carry out the plan, not the leaders who direct it.
15. Policy element details from the Elements of Crimes (intro to Art 7, para 3 and fn 6): a policy requires that the State or organisation actively promote or encourage the attack. It may 'in exceptional circumstances' be implemented by a deliberate failure to act consciously aimed at encouraging the attack, but cannot be inferred solely from the absence of action. It need not be formally adopted and may be inferred from repetition, preparation or collective mobilisation (Katanga 2014); policy and 'systematic' are separate questions (Katanga; Gbagbo). Duterte (PTC I, Apr 2026): an organisational policy of the Davao Death Squad became a State policy during the presidency.

## What the lecturers stressed

- Notion (Heinsch): crimes committed on a massive scale against a civilian population. The typical case is mass murder, but there is no need to prove intent to destroy a whole group, and political and other groups are covered. Many modes: murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation, torture, rape, enforced disappearance, apartheid.
- IMT Art 6(c): murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation and other inhumane acts against any civilian population, before or during the war, or persecutions on political, racial or religious grounds, 'in execution of or in connection with' another IMT crime — a war nexus created for the Nazi crimes against Jews. Today no armed-conflict nexus exists.
- ICTY Art 5 kept an armed conflict requirement as a jurisdictional limit only (Tadić 1995 para 141: custom requires no nexus); ICTR Art 3 required no armed conflict but did require discriminatory grounds for the attack (national, political, ethnic, racial, religious); individual discriminatory intent is needed only for persecution (Akayesu Appeal paras 464–469). In both: the act must form part of the attack and the perpetrator must know it (Kunarac Appeal paras 82–86, 99).
- Structure under Rome Art 7: objective element = chapeau (widespread or systematic attack against any civilian population) plus a listed act; subjective element = intent and knowledge, including of the context.
- Attack (Art 7(2)(a)): a course of conduct involving multiple commission of listed acts against a civilian population pursuant to or in furtherance of a State or organisational policy. Not necessarily military; a single act by one perpetrator suffices if it is part of the overall attack.
- Widespread is quantitative (victims, geography); systematic is qualitative (organised, improbable as random). Kunarac Appeal paras 90–100: alternatives; the attack as a whole must meet the threshold; the civilian population must be the primary target but need not be everyone in the area. Discussion: one killing of a civilian during a campaign of expulsions and killings can be a CAH.
- Policy: no formal programme needed — planned, directed or organised as opposed to spontaneous, isolated acts; need not be explicit. Heinsch stresses it was originally not part of customary law.
- Kenya (ICC PTC II, 31 March 2010, paras 84–93): an organisation need not be State-like; capacity matters — leadership, resources, ability to conduct a widespread or systematic attack — assessed case by case. Judge Kaul's dissent required State-like organisations. Note it was an authorisation decision at the 'reasonable basis' threshold. Discussion: could a powerful criminal cartel commit CAH? What facts would you need?
- Listed acts, with the lecture's glosses: murder (unlawfully and intentionally causing death), extermination (killing on a large scale), enslavement (ownership of a person), deportation or forcible transfer ('ethnic cleansing'), imprisonment or other severe deprivation of liberty in violation of PIL (arbitrary), torture (no official-capacity requirement), sexual violence, persecution, enforced disappearance, apartheid, other inhumane acts.

## Cases and materials

### Kunarac (Appeals)
*ICTY · 2002*

Defined widespread or systematic, the civilian population as primary object, and knowledge. A plan or policy is not a legal element under customary law (but is evidentially relevant).

**Use it for:** The chapeau elements.

### Situation in Kenya (Art 15 decision)
*ICC PTC II · 2010*

The majority held that an 'organisational policy' can come from groups with the capability to perform acts infringing on basic human values, not only State-like entities. Judge Kaul's dissent called for State-like organisations.

**Use it for:** The policy element; the limits of CAH.

### Ongwen
*ICC · 2021 (Trial) / 2022 (Appeals)*

Convicted of 61 counts including forced marriage as an 'other inhumane act' (CAH) and forced pregnancy, the first ICC conviction for it. Ongwen was a former child soldier himself (the victim–perpetrator dilemma).

**Use it for:** Other inhumane acts; sexual and gender-based crimes.

### Al Hassan
*ICC · 2024*

Convicted for CAH and war crimes in Timbuktu, including persecution on religious grounds. The Chamber split on gender persecution.

**Use it for:** Persecution; gender.

### Prosecutor v Duterte (confirmation of charges)
*ICC PTC I · 2026*

On 23 Apr 2026, 3 counts of murder and attempted murder as CAH were confirmed (49 incidents, 78 victims; Davao and national 'war on drugs', Nov 2011–Mar 2019) on a common plan with co-perpetrators. Fit to stand trial (TC III, 28 Sept 2026); trial opens 30 Nov 2026.

**Use it for:** State policy and widespread or systematic attack; a former Head of State.

### Said (trial judgment)
*ICC Trial Chamber VI · 2026*

On 23 Sept 2026 Seleka commander Mahamat Said Abdel Kani was convicted of CAH (imprisonment, torture, other inhumane acts, persecution) at a Bangui detention centre in 2013, and acquitted of the war crimes counts.

**Use it for:** CAH without a war-crimes conviction; detention-related persecution.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>Open the questions (answers are folded under each)</summary>

**Q. What are the five contextual elements of CAH (Rome Art 7)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) An attack, a course of conduct with multiple commission of acts; (2) directed against any civilian population; (3) widespread or systematic; (4) pursuant to a State or organisational policy; (5) the perpetrator's knowledge of the attack and that the conduct is part of it.

</details>

**Q. What are the elements of persecution?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A severe deprivation of fundamental rights contrary to IL; targeting a group or collectivity by reason of identity on prohibited grounds (including gender); in connection with another Art 7 act or ICC crime; discriminatory intent.

</details>

**Q. What is the function of 'other inhumane acts'?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A residual category for acts of similar character and gravity causing great suffering or serious injury to body or mental or physical health (e.g. forced marriage: AFRC, Ongwen). It must respect legality (nullum crimen).

</details>

**Q. Why does a CAH convention matter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

There is no treaty obliging States to prevent and punish CAH, unlike genocide (1948) and grave breaches (1949). The ILC draft would establish duties to prevent, criminalise, and extradite or prosecute, plus inter-State cooperation. The UNGA set negotiations for 2028–29.

</details>

**Q. How would you charge Duterte's 'war on drugs'?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

As charged and confirmed (PTC I, 23 Apr 2026): three counts of murder and attempted murder as CAH (49 incidents, 78 victims), a widespread and systematic attack on a civilian population (alleged drug users and the urban poor) under State or organisational policy (speeches, the DDS, police quotas and rewards), with co-perpetration via a common plan. ICC jurisdiction covers 1 Nov 2011–16 Mar 2019 (Art 127(2), Appeals 22 Apr 2026). Trial from 30 Nov 2026.

</details>

**Q. How did the nexus requirement for CAH evolve?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

IMT Art 6(c): connection with another IMT crime (war). CCL No. 10 dropped it. ICTY Art 5: armed conflict as a jurisdictional limit only (Tadić: custom needs no nexus). ICTR Art 3: no armed conflict, but discriminatory grounds for the attack. Rome Art 7: no armed conflict, no general discrimination requirement, but a State or organisational policy.

</details>

**Q. Can a single killing be a CAH?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Yes, if it forms part of a widespread or systematic attack against a civilian population and the perpetrator knows this (Kunarac Appeal paras 90–100). The attack as a whole, not the individual act, must be widespread or systematic.

</details>

**Q. What would reopening the CAH definition in the new convention risk, and what is proposed?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Proposals: list forced marriage and gender apartheid as separate crimes, add the slave trade, starvation and reproductive violence; drop or keep the Art 7(3) gender definition; delete the persecution 'connection' requirement. Risks: some States want a cumulative 'widespread and systematic' chapeau, a link to armed conflict, immunity carve-outs, and they question jus cogens status. The Sixth Committee's consensus habit lets a few States block (Sadat 2024).

</details>

**Q. Rome Art 7(2)(i) versus ICPPED: what extra does the CAH of enforced disappearance require?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Intent to remove the person from the protection of the law for a prolonged period; a State or political organisation behind it (by, or with its authorisation, support or acquiescence); and the CAH chapeau (part of a widespread or systematic attack, with knowledge). ICPPED needs none of the intent or duration elements, and only State agents or those acting with State acquiescence; one case is enough for a violation.

</details>
