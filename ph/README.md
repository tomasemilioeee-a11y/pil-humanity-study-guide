## Answer method

1. Budget the three hours: about 50 minutes per essay and 70 minutes for the problem, plus reading time. Answer exactly two essays.
2. Problem: answer each sub-question under its own heading. Start by classifying the situation (peace, IAC, NIAC, internationalised, occupation) with the test and the facts.
3. Then the applicable law: which IHRL treaties, and does jurisdiction reach extraterritorially (spatial, personal, functional)? Which IHL rules? Any valid derogation?
4. Per right or rule: state the IHRL test and the IHL test and how they co-apply (lex specialis versus harmonious interpretation, Hassan, GC 36 para 64). Use the facts' own words.
5. Individual criminal responsibility where relevant: which crime (contextual plus specific elements), mode of liability (Arts 25, 28), and the forum (ICC Arts 12–13, 17, 27, 127; universal jurisdiction).
6. Essays (750 words): a one-sentence thesis, three or four arguments each anchored in a case, provision or reading, a counter-argument, and a critical conclusion (humanity, effectiveness, fragmentation, selectivity).
7. Cite in brackets (ANS doesn't allow footnotes): article numbers always; cases and authors for extra points.

### The Northern Corridor (80 min)

Since 2024, the Free Morava Army (FMA), an armed group with a command structure, uniforms and a radio station, has fought the government of Kestria in its northern provinces. Clashes occur weekly, and about 200,000 people are displaced. Neighbouring Valdora, a party to the ECHR like Kestria, supplies the FMA with weapons, pays its commanders and has officers who help plan its major offensives. In 2026, Valdora's troops cross the border and take control of the town of Lenz, setting up checkpoints and appointing a civilian mayor. At a Lenz checkpoint, Valdoran soldiers shoot dead an unarmed teenager who ran towards them, and later detain 30 local men 'for security reasons' for four months without any review. Valdoran drones also strike an FMA-held warehouse in Kestria that Valdora claims was used by a Kestrian commander, killing 12 civilians sheltering there. FMA fighters in Lenz force ten women into 'marriages' with commanders. Kestria is a party to the Rome Statute; Valdora is not. Analyse the legal situation.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Classify the conflict(s): Kestria–FMA as a NIAC (intensity and organisation: Tadić, Haradinaj). Internationalisation through Valdora's overall control (Tadić Appeals) and its direct intervention, so an IAC between Kestria and Valdora.
- Occupation of Lenz: effective control under Art 42 HR (checkpoints, an appointed mayor), so the GCIV regime and the occupant's Art 43 duties apply.
- IHRL applicability: Valdora's jurisdiction under Art 1 ECHR (spatial, through effective control over Lenz: Al-Skeini, Loizidou; personal, through checkpoints and custody: Jaloud). The drone strike in active hostilities (Georgia v Russia II versus Carter).
- The checkpoint shooting: law enforcement paradigm in occupation (GC 36, McCann, absolute necessity); IHL (is the teenager a civilian, any DPH? no). The duty to investigate (Al-Skeini, Jaloud).
- Detention of 30 men: GCIV 78 imperative security reasons with periodic review (six months, plus an initial appeal). Art 5 ECHR and Hassan (no review means arbitrary). Derogation?
- The drone strike: distinction (is the warehouse a military objective under API 52(2)?), proportionality (12 civilians versus military advantage), precautions (API 57). A war crime under Rome 8(2)(b)(iv) in IAC.
- Forced marriages: CAH 'other inhumane acts' (Ongwen, AFRC) if part of a widespread or systematic attack with an organisational policy; war crimes in NIAC: sexual slavery (8(2)(e)(vi)) and outrages upon personal dignity (CA3, 8(2)(c)(ii)). The FMA as an organisation.
- ICC jurisdiction: territory of Kestria (Art 12(2)(a)), so jurisdiction over Valdoran nationals too. Admissibility (Art 17). Valdora's non-party status is irrelevant for territorial jurisdiction.
- State responsibility of Valdora: attribution of FMA conduct (effective control versus overall control; Bosnia), CA1, and the duty to investigate.

</details>

### Operation Clean Streets (70 min)

President Ramos of Solera (a party to the ICCPR, its First Optional Protocol and, until last year, the Rome Statute) announced a 'war on drug pushers', promising police 'immunity for anyone who kills them'. Over three years, police and unidentified 'vigilantes', often linked to police units, killed about 7,000 people in poor neighbourhoods, frequently after their names appeared on police 'watch lists'. Police reports say most victims 'fought back' (nanlaban). Solera declared a state of emergency, notified the UN, and suspended habeas corpus for drug suspects. Solera's withdrawal from the Rome Statute became effective eight months ago; the ICC Prosecutor had opened a preliminary examination two years before and requested authorisation to investigate one month before withdrawal took effect, granted two months after. Ramos is no longer in office and has been arrested and surrendered to the ICC. His lawyers argue (1) the ICC has no jurisdiction after withdrawal, (2) Solera's own Department of Justice is reviewing 50 cases, so the case is inadmissible, (3) these were police operations, not an 'attack' against civilians, and (4) Ramos is unfit to stand trial. Families also want to go to the UN Human Rights Committee. Advise the families and the Prosecutor.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Jurisdiction after withdrawal: Art 127, crimes committed while Solera was a party remain within jurisdiction; the authorisation here came two months after withdrawal took effect, so unlike Burundi (authorised before) it was not 'in time'. Jurisdiction rests on Art 127(2): the preliminary examination, opened two years earlier, was a 'matter under consideration by the Court' before withdrawal took effect (Duterte, Appeals Chamber 2026, as recorded in this file's ICC topic). The 2023 Appeals judgment did not decide this point..
- Admissibility (Art 17): the same person and substantially the same conduct test. A review of 50 low-level police cases does not mirror the case against Ramos (the Philippines deferral decision); unwilling or unable assessment; gravity.
- CAH contextual elements: the attack (multiple commission of killings), against a civilian population (suspected users and the urban poor are civilians, not combatants: no armed conflict), widespread (7,000) and systematic (watch lists, rewards, patterns), State policy (speeches, promises of immunity), knowledge.
- Underlying crimes: murder (7(1)(a)); possibly imprisonment, torture, other inhumane acts; persecution of the poor? (Is it a prohibited ground? Probably 'other grounds' is contested.)
- Modes of liability for Ramos: indirect co-perpetration, ordering or inducing (Art 25(3)(b)), and Art 28(b) superior responsibility of civilians. Head-of-State immunity is irrelevant (Art 27, and he is a former official).
- Fitness to stand trial: the ability to meaningfully participate (understand charges, instruct counsel); the Court's assessment through medical experts; accommodations versus a stay of proceedings (Ongwen, Gbagbo; the Duterte fitness decision).
- IHRL route: ICCPR Art 6 (arbitrary deprivation of life, GC 36; the State's duty to protect from vigilantes and to investigate; 'nanlaban' as an implausible narrative), Art 9 (arbitrary detention).
- Derogation: Art 4 requires a public emergency threatening the life of the nation (doubtful); habeas corpus is protected even in emergencies (GC 29, GC 35); Art 6 and Art 7 are non-derogable.
- HRC admissibility: victim status (families as victims); exhaustion of domestic remedies (ineffective if no investigations); the same matter not being examined elsewhere (the ICC is not 'another procedure of international investigation' for this purpose).

</details>

### The siege of Haldin (70 min)

During an international armed conflict between Arvia and Bessmar, Arvian forces encircle the Bessmari city of Haldin (population 300,000). The Arvian commander, General Toll, orders that 'nothing enters, nothing leaves' until the city surrenders. Arvian forces destroy the city's water treatment plant, which also served a Bessmari army barracks. UN agencies request access for food convoys; Arvia refuses, citing security concerns that weapons could be smuggled. Arvian officials publicly describe Haldin's population as 'animals who all support terrorism'. After six months, hundreds of civilians, mainly children, have died of malnutrition. In the territory Arvia occupies near Haldin, captured Bessmari men are held in a sports hall, beaten during interrogation and subjected to mock executions. Arvia is a party to the ECHR, the Genocide Convention and the Rome Statute; Bessmar is a party to the Genocide Convention and the ECHR. Discuss the responsibility of Arvia and of General Toll.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Applicable law: IAC (CA2), API (if a party), customary IHL. ECHR jurisdiction over a siege (spatial or functional? Georgia v Russia II's limits) and over detainees (personal: authority and control, Al-Skeini).
- Starvation: API 54(1), the prohibition of starvation as a method of warfare; 54(2), objects indispensable to survival (the water plant) unless used solely for the armed forces or in direct support (dual use, with exceptions in 54(3) still barring action that leaves civilians starving).
- Humanitarian access: API 70; GCIV 23 (free passage); consent can't be arbitrarily withheld; the right to verify consignments as a less restrictive alternative to refusal.
- Attack on the water plant: is it a military objective (dual use)? Proportionality; API 54(2) as lex specialis protection.
- Ill-treatment of detainees: torture and inhuman treatment (CAT Art 1 elements; mock executions as mental torture); GCIII and GCIV protections; grave breaches (torture, inhuman treatment); Art 3 ECHR (absolute; Selmouni; Gäfgen).
- General Toll's individual responsibility: the war crime of starvation (8(2)(b)(xxv)); attacks on civilian objects; torture as a war crime and grave breach; ordering (25(3)(b)) and command responsibility (Art 28) for detainee abuse.
- CAH? Extermination (7(1)(b), including deprivation of food 'calculated to bring about destruction'), murder, other inhumane acts: a widespread and systematic attack on the civilian population of Haldin; policy.
- Genocide? Art II(c) conditions of life. Dehumanising statements as evidence of intent, but is destruction 'the only reasonable inference' versus a military aim to force surrender (Croatia v Serbia; South Africa v Israel debates)? The protected group (national: Bessmari).
- State responsibility: Arvia's breaches of IHL and the ECHR, the Genocide Convention duty to prevent; Bessmar's interstate application (ECHR Art 33; ICJ under Genocide Convention Art IX); third States' CA1 duties (arms suppliers).

</details>

## Practice essay questions

1. After Georgia v Russia (II), is the 'context of chaos' doctrine a principled limit on the ECtHR's jurisdiction or an abdication of its role in armed conflict? Consider Carter v Russia and Ukraine and the Netherlands v Russia. *(Lecture 7 discussion)*
2. Should the views of UN treaty bodies be legally binding? Are individual complaints procedures effective remedies? *(Lecture 9 discussion)*
3. Is the neo-imperial critique of the ICC primarily a defect of the Court, its legal design, or the States around it? *(Lecture 11 discussion)*
4. 'Lex specialis is dead: co-application through harmonious interpretation is the only coherent approach to IHL and IHRL.' Discuss with reference to Hassan and General Comment 36.
5. Critically assess the ECtHR's approach to extraterritorial jurisdiction in times of armed conflict after Georgia v Russia (II) and Ukraine and the Netherlands v Russia.
6. Is the 'only reasonable inference' standard for genocidal intent too demanding? Discuss with reference to the ICJ and ad hoc tribunal jurisprudence.
7. Does the crime of aggression's jurisdictional regime at the ICC undermine the claim that aggression is 'the supreme international crime'?
8. To what extent is 'humanity' a legal concept rather than a moral aspiration in international law?
9. 'The International Criminal Court is facing an existential crisis.' Discuss, with reference to withdrawals, sanctions and non-cooperation.
10. How effective are the mechanisms for enforcing IHL compared with those for IHRL? Consider Common Article 1 and the failure of the ICRC–Swiss initiative.
11. Should the prohibition of torture admit any exceptions? Discuss the 'ticking bomb' scenario in light of international case law.
12. Evaluate the contribution of crimes against humanity's 'other inhumane acts' and 'persecution' categories to capturing gendered harm.
13. Can international law keep humanity in the loop? Assess the legal framework governing autonomous weapons systems.
14. Has the ICJ in The Gambia v Myanmar (Merits, 19 November 2026) lowered the threshold for proving a State's genocidal intent, or merely clarified it? Discuss with reference to Bosnia (2007), Croatia (2015), the interveners' arguments and the joint dissent. *(Lecture 19 — must-cite jurisprudence)*
