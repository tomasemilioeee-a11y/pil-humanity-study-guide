## Answer method

1. Budget the three hours: about 50 minutes per essay and 70 minutes for the problem, plus reading time. Answer exactly two essays.
2. Problem: answer each sub-question under its own heading. Start by classifying the situation (peace, IAC, NIAC, internationalised, occupation) with the test and the facts.
3. Then the applicable law: which IHRL treaties, and does jurisdiction reach extraterritorially (spatial, personal, functional)? Which IHL rules? Any valid derogation?
4. Per right or rule: state the IHRL test and the IHL test and how they co-apply (lex specialis versus harmonious interpretation, Hassan, GC 36 para 64). Use the facts' own words.
5. Individual criminal responsibility where relevant: which crime (contextual plus specific elements), mode of liability (Arts 25, 28), and the forum (ICC Arts 12–13, 17, 27, 127; universal jurisdiction).
6. Essays (750 words): a one-sentence thesis, three or four arguments each anchored in a case, provision or reading, a counter-argument, and a critical conclusion (humanity, effectiveness, fragmentation, selectivity).
7. Cite in brackets (ANS doesn't allow footnotes): article numbers always; cases and authors for extra points.

### ★ Colville's 'war on narco-terrorism' (70 min)

*Sample IHRL exam (Powderly, 11 March 2026), Part A, with the official answer key. Retold in our own words; the original is on Brightspace.*

Colville, a party to the ICCPR and the ECHR, elects a populist President, Laura Kaldor, in December 2025 on promises to rid the country of drugs and push migrants back; she vows a drug-free society by any means. On 3 January 2026 her government creates the Drug and Immigration Prevention Service (DIPS) and announces by executive order that its agents face no operational limits: they may arrest, detain, deport and use lethal force, and a new law will give all DIPS staff immunity from prosecution. Kaldor calls it a declaration of war.

From 4 January DIPS patrols the capital, Goni. It arrests hundreds of people, mostly homeless people suspected of addiction, makes random stops and searches, and shoots several people who run from arrest, three fatally. Detainees are held without charge in improvised centres in sports halls, with only sporadic access to lawyers.

On 5 February the navy, on Kaldor's authorisation, sinks four boats suspected of carrying drugs from neighbouring Bass (two flagged in Colville, two in Bass), killing 17 people believed to be Bass nationals. On 7 February it strikes 'drug manufacturing sites' inside Bass, killing about 12. Bass protests the violation of its sovereignty but admits that drug groups control some no-go areas near the border. Kaldor replies that Colville is at war with 'narco-terrorists' sheltering in Bass and may strike them anywhere under the laws of war.

On 8 February largely peaceful mass protests begin, and opposition leader Michael Chinkin addresses the crowd in Goni. On 9 February all gatherings of more than 20 people are banned, citing a 'war on our streets'. Police break up gatherings and charge participants with administrative offences punishable by a fine or up to six months in prison. Chinkin is arrested three days running and, after a short hearing at which his lawyer could not cross-examine the arresting officer, is sentenced to six months.

As UN Special Rapporteur, advise the Human Rights Council on (1) the lawfulness of the lethal force used by DIPS and the navy (30 points) and (2) the other IHRL issues raised by Colville's 'war on narco-terrorism', including its response to the opposition (30 points). Bonus points for further issues and a clear structure.

<details><summary>Official marking key (summarised, with points)</summary>

- OFFICIAL KEY Q1, framework (15 pts): when lethal force may be used — in armed conflict against combatants (IAC), fighters (NIAC) or civilians directly participating (2); outside armed conflict only as a last resort in the most exceptional circumstances (2); absolutely necessary and proportionate, with the tests (2); 'arbitrary deprivation of life' under IHRL instruments (2); case law and authorities — McCann, GC 36 (2). Is there an armed conflict? Tadić definition, organisation and intensity, applied to the facts (3); conclusion: IHRL is the applicable framework (2).
- OFFICIAL KEY Q1, application (15 pts): the authorisation of lethal force and blanket immunity for DIPS (3); extraterritorial scope of Colville's obligations for the boat strikes, with case law — Al-Skeini, Carter, GC 36 para 63, Georgia v Russia II (6); force not absolutely necessary or proportionate, with case law (4); procedural obligations: the duty to investigate (2).
- OFFICIAL KEY Q2, assembly and association (15 pts): ECHR Art 11 and ICCPR Arts 21–22 (3); a core freedom interconnected with expression and religion (1); central to pluralism and democracy (1); permissible grounds for limitation — prescribed by law, legitimate aim, necessary in a democratic society (5); positive obligations to facilitate and protect peaceful assemblies (5).
- OFFICIAL KEY Q2, liberty and application: freedom from arbitrary deprivation of liberty — ICCPR 9, ECHR 5 (5); applying assembly, association, expression and liberty to the facts — the blanket 20-person ban, mass dispersals, administrative sanctions, detention without charge (10); Chinkin's repeated arrests, the unfair hearing (ICCPR 14, ECHR 6) and abuse of limitations to silence the opposition (Art 18 ECHR: Navalnyy, Merabishvili) (5).
- Other relevant points: derogation (Art 4 ICCPR, Art 15 ECHR — no proclaimed emergency; 'war' rhetoric is not a derogation), discrimination against homeless people and migrants, refoulement and deportations, the 'war' label not creating an armed conflict (compare the 2025 boat strikes debate: Schmitt and Milanovic).

</details>

### ★ The Norland–Estavia conflict (75 min)

*Sample IHL exam (Heinsch, 30 June 2026), Part A. Retold in our own words; the original is on Brightspace.*

Norland and Estavia are neighbours and parties to the four Geneva Conventions and both 1977 Additional Protocols. The People's Liberation Movement (PLM), an organised armed group seeking autonomy for a linguistic minority in eastern Norland, holds several towns and has fought Norland's forces for years. Estavia secretly gives it weapons, intelligence, training and communications equipment.

In February 2027 Norland launches an offensive against PLM territory. Within weeks Estavian special forces are fighting alongside the PLM, and Estavian artillery fires in support from across the border.

On 18 March, without warning, Norland destroys most of the Al-Hayat water treatment plant in a PLM-held city, which supplies about 200,000 civilians, saying the PLM used its communications systems; severe water shortages follow. Norland also uses an AI targeting tool, 'Aegis-X', which flags suspected fighters from communications and behavioural data. On 25 March, after a brief human check, a drone strikes a gathering near a school that the tool flagged as a meeting of PLM commanders, killing about 120 people; many turn out to be civilians, and the tool relied on outdated data.

The PLM captures 15 uniformed Norlandic soldiers who were carrying arms openly and hands them to Estavia, which shows them on television and social media.

By June 2027 Estavia's regular army has effective control of several eastern districts and runs them through a military administration; civilians lack food, medicine, fuel and electricity. Estavia lets the ICRC and the UN deliver some food but blocks fuel and communications equipment, citing military necessity. In a mountain area outside PLM control, villagers spontaneously take up arms against the advancing Estavian army, carrying weapons openly but without uniforms or a command structure; those captured claim POW status.

Using IHL only (no jus ad bellum): classify the conflict or conflicts, identify the applicable law, and apply the Geneva Conventions and Additional Protocols with precise references.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Classification: Norland–PLM is a NIAC (intensity: sustained operations; organisation and territorial control), so CA3 and APII apply (APII Art 1(1): responsible command, territorial control, sustained and concerted operations). Estavia's support before February: overall control (Tadić Appeals para 137)? Weapons, intelligence and training alone are not enough. Estavian special forces and cross-border artillery mean direct intervention, so an IAC between Norland and Estavia (CA2): double qualification or full internationalisation.
- From June 2027: belligerent occupation of eastern districts (Hague Regs Art 42; CA2(2); GCIV Arts 47–78) — effective control and a military administration.
- Water facility: dual-use object. Military objective test (API 52(2)): does the communications use make an effective contribution and offer a definite military advantage? Objects indispensable to survival (API 54(2); APII 14 in NIAC) — drinking-water installations are protected unless used solely for the armed forces or in direct support, and even then not if leaving civilians without water (54(3)(b)). Proportionality (51(5)(b)); precautions: warnings (57(2)(c)), choice of means. War crimes analysis (Art 8(2)(b)(iv), (xxv) in IAC; (e)(xix) starvation in NIAC).
- Aegis-X strike: distinction (API 48, 51(2)); verification duty (57(2)(a)(i): do everything feasible; outdated data and a brief human review); presumption of civilian status in doubt (50(1)); proportionality; the school's protection (52(3) presumption). Meaningful human control, Art 36 weapons review. Responsibility of commanders and system operators.
- Captured soldiers: the PLM is a party to a NIAC, so no POW status under GCIII in that relationship, but CA3 and APII Arts 4–5 humane treatment. Once transferred to Estavia (an IAC party), are they POWs held by Estavia (GCIII Art 4A(1); Art 12 transfer rules)? Public display violates GCIII Art 13(2) (protection from insults and public curiosity), and outrages on dignity (CA3(1)(c)).
- Relief in occupied territory: occupant's duty to ensure supplies (GCIV 55–56) and to agree to relief schemes when the population is inadequately supplied (GCIV 59: 'shall agree' and facilitate); limited consignment checks allowed (59, 61); refusing fuel and water-purification equipment cannot be justified by generic military necessity. Starvation and objects indispensable to survival (API 54; 70).
- Levée en masse (GCIII Art 4A(6); Hague Regs Art 2): inhabitants of non-occupied territory who spontaneously take up arms on the enemy's approach, carrying arms openly and respecting the laws of war, are entitled to POW status without uniforms or command structure. Art 5 GCIII tribunal in case of doubt.

</details>

### ★ Yeonpyeong and the Cheonan: OTP memo (35 min)

*Sample ICL exam (Heinsch), Question 4. Retold in our own words; the original is on Brightspace.*

In December 2010 the ICC Prosecutor opened a preliminary examination into two 2010 incidents on South Korean territory: North Korea's shelling of Yeonpyeong Island on 23 November, which killed South Korean marines and civilians and wounded many others, and the sinking of the warship Cheonan on 26 March, allegedly by a torpedo from a North Korean submarine, which killed 46.

As a legal officer in the Office of the Prosecutor, write a short memo: what must the Prosecutor examine, and under which provisions, before opening a case; which crimes should be considered; and how good are the chances, given that North Korea is not a party and South Korea ratified the Statute on 13 November 2002?

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Preliminary examination and Art 53(1): reasonable basis that a crime within jurisdiction was committed; admissibility (complementarity, gravity); interests of justice. Trigger: proprio motu (Art 15) needs PTC authorisation.
- Jurisdiction: temporal (after 1 Feb 2003 for South Korea, Art 11(2)); territorial (Art 12(2)(a): South Korean territory, including the island and the vessel — flag-State jurisdiction for the warship); nationality of North Korean perpetrators is irrelevant if territorial jurisdiction exists.
- Was there an armed conflict? An IAC between the two Koreas (CA2: any resort to armed force between States; the 1953 armistice, no peace treaty). Contextual elements of war crimes and the nexus.
- Crimes: (a) Yeonpyeong — intentionally directing attacks against civilians or civilian objects (8(2)(b)(i), (ii)); disproportionate attack (8(2)(b)(iv)); attacks on marines are attacks on lawful military objectives, so not a war crime per se. (b) Cheonan — a warship and crew are military objectives, so no war crime unless perfidy or other prohibited means; possibly aggression, but no ICC jurisdiction in 2010 (and Art 15 bis(5) excludes non-party nationals).
- Admissibility and gravity: limited scale (Comoros-type analysis); no genuine national proceedings possible against North Korean officials. Outcome: the actual Prosecutor closed the examination in 2014 — Cheonan targeted a military objective; Yeonpyeong lacked evidence of intent to target civilians or clearly excessive harm.

</details>

### The Northern Corridor (80 min)

*Original practice problem.*

Since 2024, the Free Morava Army (FMA), an armed group with a command structure, uniforms and a radio station, has fought the government of Kestria in its northern provinces. Clashes occur weekly, and about 200,000 people are displaced. Neighbouring Valdora, a party to the ECHR like Kestria, supplies the FMA with weapons, pays its commanders and has officers who help plan its major offensives. In 2026, Valdora's troops cross the border and take control of the town of Lenz, setting up checkpoints and appointing a civilian mayor. At a Lenz checkpoint, Valdoran soldiers shoot dead an unarmed teenager who ran towards them, and later detain 30 local men 'for security reasons' for four months without any review. Valdoran drones also strike an FMA-held warehouse in Kestria that Valdora claims was used by a Kestrian commander, killing 12 civilians sheltering there. FMA fighters in Lenz force ten women into 'marriages' with commanders. Kestria is a party to the Rome Statute; Valdora is not. Analyse the legal situation.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Classify the conflict(s): Kestria–FMA as a NIAC (intensity and organisation: Tadić, Haradinaj). Internationalisation through Valdora's overall control (Tadić Appeals) and its direct intervention, so an IAC between Kestria and Valdora.
- Occupation of Lenz: effective control under Art 42 HR (checkpoints, an appointed mayor), so the GCIV regime and the occupant's Art 43 duties apply.
- IHRL applicability: Valdora's jurisdiction under Art 1 ECHR (spatial, through effective control over Lenz: Al-Skeini, Loizidou; personal, through checkpoints and custody: Jaloud). The drone strike in active hostilities (Georgia v Russia II versus Carter).
- The checkpoint shooting: law enforcement paradigm in occupation (GC 36, McCann, absolute necessity); IHL (is the teenager a civilian, any DPH? no). The duty to investigate (Al-Skeini, Jaloud).
- Detention of 30 men: GCIV 78 imperative security reasons with periodic review (six months, plus an initial appeal). Art 5 ECHR and Hassan (no review means arbitrary). Derogation?
- The drone strike: distinction (is the warehouse a military objective under API 52(2)?), proportionality (12 civilians versus military advantage), precautions (API 57). A war crime under Rome 8(2)(b)(iv) in IAC.
- Forced marriages: CAH 'other inhumane acts' (Ongwen, AFRC) if part of a widespread or systematic attack with an organisational policy; war crimes in NIAC: sexual slavery (8(2)(e)(vi)) and outrages upon personal dignity (CA3, 8(2)(c)(ii)). The FMA as an organisation.
- ICC jurisdiction: territory of Kestria (Art 12(2)(a)), so jurisdiction over Valdoran nationals too. Admissibility (Art 17). Valdora's non-party status is irrelevant for territorial jurisdiction.
- State responsibility of Valdora: attribution of FMA conduct (effective control versus overall control; Bosnia), CA1, and the duty to investigate.

</details>

### Operation Clean Streets (70 min)

*Original practice problem.*

President Ramos of Solera (a party to the ICCPR, its First Optional Protocol and, until last year, the Rome Statute) announced a 'war on drug pushers', promising police 'immunity for anyone who kills them'. Over three years, police and unidentified 'vigilantes', often linked to police units, killed about 7,000 people in poor neighbourhoods, frequently after their names appeared on police 'watch lists'. Police reports say most victims 'fought back' (nanlaban). Solera declared a state of emergency, notified the UN, and suspended habeas corpus for drug suspects. Solera's withdrawal from the Rome Statute became effective eight months ago; the ICC Prosecutor had opened a preliminary examination two years before and requested authorisation to investigate one month before withdrawal took effect, granted two months after. Ramos is no longer in office and has been arrested and surrendered to the ICC. His lawyers argue (1) the ICC has no jurisdiction after withdrawal, (2) Solera's own Department of Justice is reviewing 50 cases, so the case is inadmissible, (3) these were police operations, not an 'attack' against civilians, and (4) Ramos is unfit to stand trial. Families also want to go to the UN Human Rights Committee. Advise the families and the Prosecutor.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Jurisdiction after withdrawal: Art 127, crimes committed while Solera was a party remain within jurisdiction; the authorisation here came two months after withdrawal took effect, so unlike Burundi (authorised before) it was not 'in time'. Jurisdiction rests on Art 127(2): the preliminary examination, opened two years earlier, was a 'matter under consideration by the Court' before withdrawal took effect (Duterte, Appeals Chamber 2026, as recorded in this file's ICC topic). The 2023 Appeals judgment did not decide this point..
- Admissibility (Art 17): the same person and substantially the same conduct test. A review of 50 low-level police cases does not mirror the case against Ramos (the Philippines deferral decision); unwilling or unable assessment; gravity.
- CAH contextual elements: the attack (multiple commission of killings), against a civilian population (suspected users and the urban poor are civilians, not combatants: no armed conflict), widespread (7,000) and systematic (watch lists, rewards, patterns), State policy (speeches, promises of immunity), knowledge.
- Underlying crimes: murder (7(1)(a)); possibly imprisonment, torture, other inhumane acts; persecution of the poor? (Is it a prohibited ground? Probably 'other grounds' is contested.)
- Modes of liability for Ramos: indirect co-perpetration, ordering or inducing (Art 25(3)(b)), and Art 28(b) superior responsibility of civilians. Head-of-State immunity is irrelevant (Art 27, and he is a former official).
- Fitness to stand trial: the ability to meaningfully participate (understand charges, instruct counsel); the Court's assessment through medical experts; accommodations versus a stay of proceedings (Ongwen, Gbagbo; the Duterte fitness decision).
- IHRL route: ICCPR Art 6 (arbitrary deprivation of life, GC 36; the State's duty to protect from vigilantes and to investigate; 'nanlaban' as an implausible narrative), Art 9 (arbitrary detention).
- Derogation: Art 4 requires a public emergency threatening the life of the nation (doubtful); habeas corpus is protected even in emergencies (GC 29, GC 35); Art 6 and Art 7 are non-derogable.
- HRC admissibility: victim status (families as victims); exhaustion of domestic remedies (ineffective if no investigations); the same matter not being examined elsewhere (the ICC is not 'another procedure of international investigation' for this purpose).

</details>

### The siege of Haldin (70 min)

*Original practice problem.*

During an international armed conflict between Arvia and Bessmar, Arvian forces encircle the Bessmari city of Haldin (population 300,000). The Arvian commander, General Toll, orders that 'nothing enters, nothing leaves' until the city surrenders. Arvian forces destroy the city's water treatment plant, which also served a Bessmari army barracks. UN agencies request access for food convoys; Arvia refuses, citing security concerns that weapons could be smuggled. Arvian officials publicly describe Haldin's population as 'animals who all support terrorism'. After six months, hundreds of civilians, mainly children, have died of malnutrition. In the territory Arvia occupies near Haldin, captured Bessmari men are held in a sports hall, beaten during interrogation and subjected to mock executions. Arvia is a party to the ECHR, the Genocide Convention and the Rome Statute; Bessmar is a party to the Genocide Convention and the ECHR. Discuss the responsibility of Arvia and of General Toll.

<details><summary>Issue checklist</summary>

- Applicable law: IAC (CA2), API (if a party), customary IHL. ECHR jurisdiction over a siege (spatial or functional? Georgia v Russia II's limits) and over detainees (personal: authority and control, Al-Skeini).
- Starvation: API 54(1), the prohibition of starvation as a method of warfare; 54(2), objects indispensable to survival (the water plant) unless used solely for the armed forces or in direct support (dual use, with exceptions in 54(3) still barring action that leaves civilians starving).
- Humanitarian access: API 70; GCIV 23 (free passage); consent can't be arbitrarily withheld; the right to verify consignments as a less restrictive alternative to refusal.
- Attack on the water plant: is it a military objective (dual use)? Proportionality; API 54(2) as lex specialis protection.
- Ill-treatment of detainees: torture and inhuman treatment (CAT Art 1 elements; mock executions as mental torture); GCIII and GCIV protections; grave breaches (torture, inhuman treatment); Art 3 ECHR (absolute; Selmouni; Gäfgen).
- General Toll's individual responsibility: the war crime of starvation (8(2)(b)(xxv)); attacks on civilian objects; torture as a war crime and grave breach; ordering (25(3)(b)) and command responsibility (Art 28) for detainee abuse.
- CAH? Extermination (7(1)(b), including deprivation of food 'calculated to bring about destruction'), murder, other inhumane acts: a widespread and systematic attack on the civilian population of Haldin; policy.
- Genocide? Art II(c) conditions of life. Dehumanising statements as evidence of intent, but is destruction 'the only reasonable inference' versus a military aim to force surrender (Croatia v Serbia; South Africa v Israel debates)? The protected group (national: Bessmari).
- State responsibility: Arvia's breaches of IHL and the ECHR, the Genocide Convention duty to prevent; Bessmar's interstate application (ECHR Art 33; ICJ under Genocide Convention Art IX); third States' CA1 duties (arms suppliers).

</details>

18. To what extent is 'humanity' a legal concept rather than a moral aspiration in international law?

20. How effective are the mechanisms for enforcing IHL compared with those for IHRL? Consider Common Article 1 and the failure of the ICRC–Swiss initiative.
21. Should the prohibition of torture admit any exceptions? Discuss the 'ticking bomb' scenario in light of international case law.
22. Evaluate the contribution of crimes against humanity's 'other inhumane acts' and 'persecution' categories to capturing gendered harm.
23. Can international law keep humanity in the loop? Assess the legal framework governing autonomous weapons systems.
24. Has the ICJ in The Gambia v Myanmar (Merits, 19 November 2026) lowered the threshold for proving a State's genocidal intent, or merely clarified it? Discuss with reference to Bosnia (2007), Croatia (2015), the interveners' arguments and the joint dissent. *(Lecture 19 — must-cite jurisprudence)*
