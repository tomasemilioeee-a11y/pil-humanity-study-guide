# Lectures 8–9: Human rights mechanisms

**Mind map:** [Human rights mechanisms](https://tomasemilioeee-a11y.github.io/pil-humanity-study-guide/mind-maps/hr-mechanisms.html), interactive, with recall mode and search.

## Rules to know cold

1. Charter-based UN system: the Human Rights Council (47 members), Universal Periodic Review (peer review of all States), Special Procedures (independent rapporteurs and working groups), Commissions of Inquiry and fact-finding missions (Syria, Myanmar, Ukraine, OPT), and the OHCHR (High Commissioner).
2. Treaty bodies (ten committees, e.g. HRC, CESCR, CAT, CRC, CEDAW, CERD): State reporting with concluding observations, individual communications (optional protocols or declarations), interstate complaints, inquiry procedures, and General Comments (authoritative but non-binding interpretations).
3. UNESCO: the right to education, culture, science, and freedom of expression (the often-forgotten limb).
4. European system: the ECtHR has compulsory jurisdiction and binding judgments (Art 46), supervised by the Committee of Ministers, with individual petition (Art 34). Subsidiarity and margin of appreciation (Protocol 15). Russia was expelled from the CoE in March 2022.
5. Inter-American system: the Commission (petitions, precautionary measures, country reports) filters cases to the Court. Broad reparations (restitution, rehabilitation, satisfaction, guarantees of non-repetition), conventionality control, and progressive advisory opinions (OC-23 environment, OC-32/25 climate emergency).
6. African system: the African Commission and the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights. Individuals and NGOs can access the Court only if the State has made an Art 34(6) declaration (several withdrawals: Rwanda, Tanzania, Benin, Côte d'Ivoire). The Charter has peoples' rights and duties.
7. Admissibility typology (Scheinin): victim status (direct, indirect, potential victims; no actio popularis before the ECtHR or HRC); exhaustion of effective domestic remedies; time limits (ECtHR four months since Protocol 15); not the same matter before another international body; ratione personae, materiae, loci, temporis; significant disadvantage (ECHR Art 35(3)(b)).
8. Third-party intervention (Art 36 ECHR): States and NGOs as amici, increasingly strategic.
9. Strategic litigation (Duffy's inaugural lecture): litigation is valuable beyond winning (agenda setting, empowerment, norm development), but the 'champagne moment' of a judgment rarely ends the struggle. Compliance and implementation are where impact happens.
10. Pushback and backlash: withdrawals (US from the HRC; Art 34(6) withdrawals), non-compliance (Russia, Turkey: Kavala, an Art 46(4) infringement), the Copenhagen and Brighton declarations, and the politicisation of mechanisms.
11. UN fact-finding before the ICJ: in Gambia v Myanmar (2026) the Court, with no ICTY-style judgments available, built its factual findings mainly on the HRC Fact-Finding Mission reports (rigorous methodology, comparable to or exceeding earlier UN reports, para 112) and the IIMM's analytical reports and witness statements ('considerable evidential weight', para 119). It disregarded their legal conclusions (para 100) and drew more liberal inferences because Myanmar refused access (paras 128-131).
12. Duffy, a 'skeptical believer' in litigation, proposes three lenses for judging strategic litigation: a high-definition lens (impact on several dimensions: victims and survivors, the law, political and social change, democracy and the rule of law), a long lens (impact before, during and long after judgment), and a wide-angle lens (litigation working alongside advocacy, education and law reform). Impact can be negative too: unimplemented wins, backlash, 'bad cases make bad law'. Guantánamo shows the ambivalence: Lobel calls Boumediene a 'pyrrhic victory', Duffy stresses its expressive value. Her image: a kaleidoscope, not a camera, and 'smaller champagne moments'.
13. African Court access (Plagis): the Art 34(6) declaration has been called the 'price' of creating the Court and is its biggest barrier; only ten States ever made one, and four withdrew (Rwanda, Tanzania, Benin, Côte d'Ivoire). In Umuhoza v Rwanda (2016) the Court held the declaration is separable from the Protocol and may be withdrawn, but only with one year's notice for legal certainty (following IACtHR Ivcher Bronstein v Peru), so pending and new cases filed in that year remain admissible. In Falana v AU dissenting judges found Art 34(6) inconsistent with the Charter, but the Court cannot set it aside.
14. The African Court's material jurisdiction is the broadest of the regional courts: Art 3(1) of the Protocol covers the Charter and 'any other relevant human rights instrument ratified by the States concerned' (e.g. the ECOWAS Democracy Protocol in APDH v Côte d'Ivoire, 2016). The UDHR may be used as custom only where the right is also in a ratified treaty (Omary v Tanzania, 2014). Applicants need not cite Charter articles (Chacha v Tanzania). The Court reviews national proceedings for manifest arbitrariness but is not an appeal court and does not apply domestic law (Abubakari v Tanzania).

## What the lecturers stressed

- Lecture 8 opened on the 80th anniversary of the Nuremberg judgment (1 October 1946).
- Human Rights Council: a subsidiary organ of the GA. Tools: UPR; Special Procedures; the confidential complaint procedure (formerly '1503') for patterns of gross violations, not individual cases; Commissions of Inquiry; an Advisory Committee of 18 experts.
- UPR: every UN member reviewed every four and a half years, 42 States a year, by a troika, on three documents (national report, UN compilation, stakeholders' summary). States 'accept' or 'note' recommendations; over 56,000 made in the first two cycles; roughly half reportedly lead to action. Non-confrontational peer review.
- Special Procedures (GA Res 60/251): independent unpaid experts, 46 thematic and 14 country mandates; communications (letters of allegation, urgent appeals), country visits, standing invitations, annual reports, collective statements.
- Treaty bodies: State reporting (with a review procedure for chronic non-reporters); inquiries by CAT, CEDAW, CESCR, CRPD and CRC into grave or systematic violations if the State accepted competence; CED has extra powers (urgent action to search for a disappeared person, referral of widespread or systematic disappearances to the GA). Inter-State complaints are possible under several treaties. The slides say none has been brought, but three were lodged under CERD Art 11 in 2018 (Qatar v Saudi Arabia, Qatar v UAE, State of Palestine v Israel), the first inter-State communications before a treaty body. Individual complaints exist for all except the CMW: written, confidential, no legal aid.
- OHCHR: called for at the 1993 Vienna World Conference, created by GA Res 48/141; the High Commissioner is appointed by the SG and approved by the GA for a renewable four-year term.
- ECtHR: individual petition was optional until Protocol 11 (1998) abolished the Commission and made the Court central; Protocol 16 (2013) allows advisory opinions. Priority categories I–VII (urgent cases first, core rights Arts 2, 3, 4, 5(1) in category III, manifestly inadmissible last). Interim measures are binding.
- Inter-American system: the American Declaration (1948) and ACHR (1969: 22 of 35 OAS States, not the US or Canada). The Commission (7 members) handles every petition first, seeks friendly settlement, issues precautionary measures and country reports. The Court (1979, 7 part-time judges; 20 States accept its contentious jurisdiction): only the Commission and States can refer cases; very influential reparations and advisory opinions.
- African system: the Banjul Charter (1981) includes collective rights and duties. The Commission (11 members, since 1987) accepts communications from NGOs and non-victims, applies exhaustion flexibly, and has biennial State reporting and its own special rapporteurs. The Court (Protocol 1998, in force 2004, Arusha, 11 judges) hears individuals and NGOs only where an Art 34(6) declaration exists (only 8 States). The ECOWAS Court does not require exhaustion. Nascent: ASEAN (2012 Declaration and Commission), the Arab and Gulf systems.
- Treaty-body admissibility (Lecture 9): direct victim, no actio popularis; ratione temporis, including continuing effects; not the same matter before another international body (the ICCPR and CED only bar simultaneous examination, allowing forum shopping, but many ECHR States have reservations); exhaustion unless unreasonably prolonged. 'Views' are not binding; follow-up rapporteurs; GA Res 68/268 (2014) on harmonising working methods. Debate: should views be binding, and are they effective remedies?
- Victim status under ECHR Art 34: person, NGO or group of individuals directly affected or at genuine risk; not 'rigid, mechanical and inflexible' (Gorraiz Lizarraga v Spain); read in light of present-day conditions (Loizidou).
- KlimaSeniorinnen (9 April 2024): building on López Ostra and Öneryıldız on environmental positive obligations, and domestic precedents (Urgenda, the German Klimabeschluss). Art 8 covers climate mitigation; Switzerland failed to do what it could (para 451 on subsidiarity). For individuals, a 'special approach' to victim status — high-intensity exposure and a pressing need for individual protection — set so high that none qualified; associations can have standing if lawfully established, dedicated to defending members' rights, and genuinely representative. The lecture's critique: individuals suffer harm, not legal entities; the reasoning leans on 'common concern of humankind'. The judgment is declaratory, leaving measures to Switzerland and the Committee of Ministers.

## Cases and materials

### Kavala v Turkey (Art 46(4))
*ECtHR (GC) · 2022*

Turkey failed to comply with the 2019 judgment ordering Kavala's release. This was the second-ever infringement procedure.

**Use it for:** The compliance gap; backlash.

### Billy et al. v Australia (Torres Strait Islanders)
*UN HRC · 2022*

Australia's failure to adapt to climate impacts violated Art 17 (home and family life) and Art 27 (minority culture). Art 6 (life) was not yet violated given the timeframe.

**Use it for:** Treaty-body communications; climate as a human rights issue.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>Open the questions (answers are folded under each)</summary>

**Q. What are the main admissibility criteria before the ECtHR?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Victim status (Art 34); exhaustion of domestic remedies; the four-month time limit; not substantially the same as a matter already examined; not manifestly ill-founded; significant disadvantage; compatibility ratione personae, materiae, loci and temporis (Art 35).

</details>

**Q. Charter-based versus treaty-based UN mechanisms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Charter-based: they derive from the UN Charter and apply to all members (HRC, UPR, Special Procedures, COIs). Treaty-based: they derive from specific treaties and bind only parties (committees: reporting, communications, General Comments).

</details>

**Q. How does access to the African Court work?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

States, the Commission and African IGOs can submit cases. Individuals and NGOs only where the State made an Art 34(6) Protocol declaration, and several States have withdrawn theirs.

</details>

**Q. What is Duffy's 'champagne moment'?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The celebration of a litigation victory. Her point is that judgments rarely end the matter: strategic litigation's impact lies in broader processes of mobilisation, implementation and norm change, so judge it beyond the courtroom.

</details>

**Q. UPR in numbers?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Every UN member every 4.5 years; 42 States a year in three two-week sessions; a troika of States; three base documents; recommendations accepted or noted; 56,000+ in the first two cycles; around half reportedly lead to action.

</details>

**Q. Who can bring a case to the Inter-American Court, and to the African Court?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Inter-American: only the Commission and States parties (individuals must go through the Commission). African: States, the African Commission and African IGOs; individuals and NGOs only against States that made an Art 34(6) declaration (only 8).

</details>

**Q. What were the victim-status criteria in KlimaSeniorinnen?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Individuals: high-intensity exposure to the adverse effects of climate change and a pressing need for individual protection (not met). Associations: lawfully established, dedicated statutory purpose of defending members' human rights against climate threats, and genuinely qualified and representative (met).

</details>

**Q. What are Duffy's three lenses and four dimensions of litigation impact?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Lenses: high-definition (detailed, multi-dimensional impact), long (before, during and long after judgment), wide-angle (litigation in context with advocacy and reform). Dimensions: victims and survivors, the law, political and social change, democracy and the rule of law. Impact can be negative (backlash, unimplemented wins). Hence 'smaller champagne moments' rather than one judgment day.

</details>

**Q. Can a State withdraw its Art 34(6) declaration, and with what effect (Umuhoza v Rwanda)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Yes: the declaration is separable from the Protocol and withdrawable at the State's discretion, but not with immediate effect. For legal certainty a one-year notice period applies (drawing on Ivcher Bronstein v Peru), during which applications can still be filed; the Court applied the same period to Tanzania (Cheusi v Tanzania 2020). Compare Rome Statute Art 127 (one year) and GC 26 on the ICCPR (no withdrawal).

</details>
