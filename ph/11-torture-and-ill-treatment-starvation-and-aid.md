# Lecture 15: Torture and ill-treatment; starvation and aid

**Mind map:** [Torture, ill-treatment, starvation and aid](https://tomasemilioeee-a11y.github.io/pil-humanity-study-guide/mind-maps/torture.html), interactive, with recall mode and search.

## Rules to know cold

1. The prohibition of torture is jus cogens and absolute (CAT; ICCPR Art 7; ECHR Art 3; Furundžija paras 153–157; Belgium v Senegal para 99). It is non-derogable and has no exceptions for terrorism or the 'ticking bomb' (Gäfgen v Germany: threatening a suspect to save a child's life was still inhuman treatment).
2. CAT Art 1: (1) severe pain or suffering, physical or mental, (2) intentionally inflicted, (3) for a purpose (information, confession, punishment, intimidation, discrimination), (4) by or with the consent or acquiescence of a public official.
3. The ECHR distinguishes torture from inhuman or degrading treatment by the severity of suffering and purpose (Ireland v UK 1978: the 'five techniques' were inhuman, not torture; Selmouni 1999: the threshold is lowered as a 'living instrument'). There is a minimum level of severity. Degrading treatment means humiliation.
4. ICL definitions: under the Rome Statute, torture as CAH (Art 7(2)(e)) requires custody or control and no purpose and no official requirement. As a war crime, the Elements require a purpose. Kunarac: no State actor requirement in ICL.
5. Rape and sexual violence can constitute torture (Aydın v Turkey; Akayesu; Furundžija; the CAT Committee). Bodily integrity.
6. Non-refoulement: absolute under Art 3 ECHR (Soering; Chahal; Saadi v Italy, no balancing against national security) and CAT Art 3. Diplomatic assurances need scrutiny (Othman).
7. 'Enhanced interrogation' (waterboarding, stress positions) is torture or inhuman treatment. Black sites and complicity (above).
8. Ill-treatment of civilians in armed conflict: prohibition of acts or threats of violence whose primary purpose is to spread terror among the civilian population (API 51(2); Galić).
9. Starvation as a method of warfare is prohibited (API 54; APII 14; ICRC customary Rule 53), and it is a war crime in IAC (Rome Art 8(2)(b)(xxv)) and since 2019 in NIAC (8(2)(e)(xix)). Protection of objects indispensable to survival.
10. Humanitarian assistance: parties must allow and facilitate rapid and unimpeded passage of relief for civilians in need (API 70; GCIV 23; the occupant's duty in GCIV 55, 59). Consent to relief operations may not be arbitrarily withheld (Oxford Guidance). Denial of aid can contribute to starvation, CAH extermination, or genocide (conditions of life).
11. Famine determinations: the IPC Famine Review Committee confirmed famine in Gaza Governorate (22 Aug 2025, conditions as of 15 Aug), the first in the Middle East, 'entirely man-made'. Israel rejected it. Famine was confirmed in El Fasher and Kadugli, Sudan, on 3 Nov 2025. Relevance: starvation as a method of warfare (API 54; Rule 53; Rome Art 8(2)(b)(xxv), and in NIAC 8(2)(e)(xix)) and the duty to allow relief (GCIV 23, 55, 59; API 70). Compare the ICJ provisional measures in South Africa v Israel.
12. Gäfgen v Germany (GC 2010) in full: (1) the threat of severe pain was inhuman treatment, not torture; (2) the officers' suspended fines did not satisfy the procedural duty to punish Art 3 violations adequately; (3) the long delay on the compensation claim breached the right to an effective remedy; but (4) no Art 6 violation, even though the body was found because of the threat, because his final confession at trial was 'voluntary' (a 'break in the causal chain'). A strong dissent said absoluteness requires excluding all evidence obtained in breach. Bantekas and Oette: affirming absoluteness is 'only the first step'; the hard question is what legal consequences follow.
13. Non-state torturers: under CAT Art 1 torture needs a public official or someone acting with official consent or acquiescence, while ICL drops this (Kunarac). Human rights bodies bend the official requirement mainly through non-refoulement: sending someone to be tortured by a de facto authority can breach CAT Art 3 (e.g. Elmi v Australia, CAT 1999, Somali factions exercising quasi-governmental control). Bantekas and Oette: criminal tribunals ascribe liability whatever the offender's status, whereas human rights bodies need an element of State involvement.

## Key passages

> No exceptional circumstances whatsoever, whether a state of war or a threat of war, internal political instability or any other public emergency, may be invoked as a justification of torture.
>
> — **Convention against Torture (1984)**, Art 2(2)

*Why it matters:* Absolute and non-derogable; jus cogens (Belgium v Senegal para 99).

## What the lecturers stressed

- The prohibition of torture and CIDT is absolute and jus cogens (ICCPR 7, ECHR 3, ACHPR 5). Three central elements in CAT Art 1: severe physical or mental suffering, a prohibited purpose, and official involvement. Torture is also a war crime (IAC and NIAC) and a crime against humanity where the context is met.
- CAT Art 2(2): no exceptional circumstances whatsoever (war, threat of war, instability, emergency) justify torture. CAT Art 15: evidence obtained by torture is inadmissible.
- Erosion after 9/11: Ben Saul — Amnesty received torture reports from over 150 States (1997–2000); US practice signalled acceptability and undermined the prohibition's universal normativity. Duffy: torture 'is not a novelty of the war on terror, it is a defining feature of it'.
- CIDT threshold: a minimum level of severity, relative to nature, context, duration, physical and mental effects, and sometimes sex, age and health (Opuz v Turkey 2009). Ireland v UK: the five techniques were inhuman and degrading, not torture. Torture is distinguished by severity and purpose.
- Positive and procedural obligations: no incommunicado detention; access to a lawyer, doctor, courts and third parties; criminalise (CAT Art 4), prosecute or extradite (Art 7), investigate promptly and impartially (Art 12), receive complaints (Art 13) and provide redress (Art 14); non-refoulement where there are substantial grounds to fear ill-treatment.
- 'Manufactured legality': the OLC memos (Gonzales, Bybee, Yoo). Bybee (1 Aug 2002): torture requires pain 'equivalent in intensity to … organ failure … or even death'. The follow-up memo listed 10 'legally acceptable' techniques: attention grasp, walling, facial hold, facial slap, cramped confinement, wall standing, stress positions, sleep deprivation, insects in a box, waterboarding. Ticking-bomb arguments; black sites.
- Extraordinary rendition (Babar Ahmad v UK (dec.), 6 July 2010, para 113; adopted in El-Masri GC 2012 para 221): an extra-judicial transfer for detention and interrogation outside the normal legal system where there is a real risk of ill-treatment. Duffy: 'the nadir of the descent to international illegality of the war on terror'. Features: authorisation at the highest level (the 17 Sept 2001 Memorandum of Notification; Cheney's 'dark side'), absolute secrecy, standardised 'enhanced interrogation', guaranteed impunity, ongoing violations, and multi-State complicity — a 'global spider's web'.
- Abu Zubaydah v Poland para 524: secret detention aimed to remove detainees from any legal protection; host-State cooperation was the 'necessary condition' for black sites. Para 511: the treatment amounted to torture. Para 512: Poland 'facilitated the whole process' through 'acquiescence and connivance' and must have known the risk. Also Al Nashiri v Poland (2014), Abu Zubaydah v Lithuania and Al Nashiri v Romania (2018), El-Masri v Macedonia (2012).
- Rights violated by rendition: liberty (unacknowledged detention is 'a complete negation' of liberty guarantees); torture and CIDT (including family members' anguish); enforced disappearance; non-refoulement (non-derogable, cannot be weighed against security; the flimsiness of diplomatic assurances — Babar Ahmad allowed extradition to a supermax with assurances); remedy and reparation; truth and the duty to investigate.
- Responses: State responsibility for aid or assistance (ARSIWA Art 16: knowledge plus a close causal link), human rights litigation, individual criminal responsibility (ICC, universal jurisdiction).

## Cases and materials

### Ireland v UK
*ECtHR · 1978*

The 'five techniques' (wall-standing, hooding, noise, sleep deprivation, food and drink deprivation) were inhuman and degrading but did not reach the 'special stigma' of torture. Revision was refused in 2018.

**Use it for:** The torture versus inhuman treatment distinction (and its evolution).

### Selmouni v France
*ECtHR (GC) · 1999*

Severe police beatings amounted to torture. The Convention is a living instrument, and increasingly high standards mean acts once classed as inhuman may now be torture.

**Use it for:** The evolving threshold.

### Gäfgen v Germany (GC)
*ECtHR · 2010*

Police threatened a kidnapping suspect with severe pain to locate the child. This was inhuman treatment and absolutely prohibited, whatever the motive.

**Use it for:** The ticking bomb and absoluteness.

### Prosecutor v Furundžija
*ICTY · 1998*

The prohibition of torture is jus cogens, with consequences including universal jurisdiction and the invalidity of amnesties. Rape can constitute torture.

**Use it for:** Jus cogens; sexual violence.

### Prosecutor v Galić
*ICTY · 2003 / 2006*

The campaign of sniping and shelling in Sarajevo was terror against the civilian population, a war crime (primary purpose to spread terror).

**Use it for:** Terrorising civilians.

### Abu Zubaydah v Poland / Al Nashiri v Poland
*ECtHR · 2014*

Poland hosted a CIA black site; the treatment amounted to torture; Poland was responsible through acquiescence and connivance, for secret detention (Art 5), and for failing to investigate (procedural Art 3); also for exposing the applicants to the risk of further ill-treatment and an unfair trial.

**Use it for:** Complicity in rendition; positive obligations.

### Opuz v Turkey
*ECtHR · 2009*

The State failed to protect a woman and her mother from repeated domestic violence despite knowledge of the risk (Arts 2, 3 and 14: gender-based discrimination). Ill-treatment must reach a relative minimum level of severity.

**Use it for:** The CIDT threshold; due diligence against private violence.

### Babar Ahmad v UK
*ECtHR · 2012*

Extradition to the US (possible supermax detention, life without parole) did not violate Art 3. The definition of extraordinary rendition ('an extra-judicial transfer … for the purposes of detention and interrogation outside the normal legal system, where there was a real risk of torture or cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment') comes from the earlier admissibility decision, Babar Ahmad v UK (dec.), 6 July 2010, para 113, which relied on US assurances (no rendition, no death penalty, no military commission). The 2012 judgment held that detention at ADX Florence and life sentences would not breach Art 3.

**Use it for:** Definition of rendition; diplomatic assurances.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>Open the questions (answers are folded under each)</summary>

**Q. What are the four elements of torture under CAT Art 1?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Severe physical or mental pain or suffering; intentionally inflicted; for a specified purpose; by, at the instigation of, or with the consent or acquiescence of a public official or person acting in an official capacity.

</details>

**Q. How do ICL definitions of torture differ from CAT?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

There is no official-capacity requirement (Kunarac). For CAH under the Rome Statute there is no purpose requirement, only custody or control. For war crimes, the Elements require a purpose.

</details>

**Q. What does Gäfgen say about the ticking bomb?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Even to save a life, threats of torture are prohibited. Art 3 is absolute and not subject to balancing.

</details>

**Q. What legal framework applies to starvation and humanitarian access?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

API 54 and APII 14 prohibit starvation as a method of warfare; Rome 8(2)(b)(xxv) and (e)(xix) make it a war crime. API 70 and GCIV 23, 55, 59 require allowing and facilitating relief. Consent may not be arbitrarily withheld.

</details>

**Q. What were the Bybee memo's torture standard and the 10 'acceptable' techniques?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Pain equivalent to that accompanying serious physical injury such as organ failure or death. The techniques: attention grasp, walling, facial hold, facial slap, cramped confinement, wall standing, stress positions, sleep deprivation, insects in a confinement box, waterboarding. Used to argue the CIA programme was not 'torture'.

</details>

**Q. How did the ECtHR hold Poland responsible in Abu Zubaydah?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Not by attributing CIA acts to Poland, but through its own conduct: it knew or must have known of the serious risk, hosted and facilitated the black site ('acquiescence and connivance', para 512), so it was responsible for torture (Art 3), secret detention (Art 5), the transfer, and failing to investigate.

</details>

**Q. What did Gäfgen decide on evidence obtained through the threat of torture, and why is it criticised?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The Grand Chamber found no Art 6 violation: the real evidence (the boy's body) was used, but the conviction rested on Gäfgen's later 'voluntary' confession at trial, which broke the causal chain. The dissent called this artificial: absoluteness requires excluding all evidence obtained in breach of Art 3. Bantekas and Oette suggest the Court may have been influenced by the prospect of a retrial and public outcry in Germany.

</details>
