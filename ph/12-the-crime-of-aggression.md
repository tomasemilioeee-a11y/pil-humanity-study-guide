# Lecture 16: The crime of aggression

## Rules to know cold

1. From Nuremberg's 'crimes against peace' ('the supreme international crime') to the Rome Statute Art 5, with the definition deferred to Kampala (2010). Activation took effect on 17 July 2018 (ASP decision of December 2017).
2. Art 8 bis(1), the crime: planning, preparation, initiation or execution, by a person in a position effectively to exercise control over or to direct the political or military action of a State, of an act of aggression which by its character, gravity and scale constitutes a manifest violation of the UN Charter.
3. Art 8 bis(2), the act of aggression: the use of armed force by a State against the sovereignty, territorial integrity or political independence of another State (from GA Res 3314). Lists invasion, bombardment, blockade, attack on armed forces, sending armed bands, and so on.
4. Leadership element: a leadership crime only. Ordinary soldiers are not liable (Dannenbaum argues soldiers fighting in illegal wars are wronged and their killing of enemies is not 'innocent').
5. State act requirement: there must be an act of aggression by a State. Non-state actors' force against States is excluded (debated).
6. 'Manifest violation' threshold: all three of character, gravity and scale must be sufficient (understanding 7). This excludes grey areas such as arguable humanitarian intervention or anticipatory self-defence.
7. Jurisdictional regime: Art 15 bis (State referral and proprio motu) excludes nationals of non-parties and crimes on their territory (Art 15 bis(5)). Parties may opt out (15 bis(4)). The 2017 ASP decision (Zimmermann; Kress) adopted the restrictive reading: no jurisdiction over nationals of States parties that haven't ratified the amendment. Art 15 ter: an SC referral has no such limits.
8. Effective inoperability: Russia isn't a party, and the SC is vetoed, so the ICC can't prosecute aggression against Ukraine. Hence the Special Tribunal for the Crime of Aggression against Ukraine: CoE–Ukraine Agreement 25 June 2025, Rada ratification 15 July 2025, Enlarged Partial Agreement on the Management Committee (CM/Res(2026)5, 15 May 2026, 36 States plus the EU), seat in The Hague. Statute: no functional immunity (Art 23(4)); a sitting troika member's indictment cannot be confirmed until they leave office or immunity is waived (Art 23(5)/25(2)); limited trials in absentia with retrial right (Art 28). The July 2025 special session failed to harmonise Art 15 bis (Res ICC-ASP/S-1/Res.1; reconvene 2029).
9. The domestic route: Ukrainian and other national prosecutions (aggression as a domestic crime), but immunity issues persist for leaders.
10. Kress contrasts Nuremberg's 'supreme international crime' (aggression) with the later case law's description of genocide as the 'crime of crimes'. The shift reflects the move from a State-sovereignty-oriented to a human-being-oriented approach (Tadić para 97). Aggression necessarily violates international peace; genocide may stay within one State. Whether Nuremberg's ranking still holds depends on whether international peace and security remains the primary value of the international legal order. Good opening for an essay on aggression's place among the core crimes.

## Key passages

> …'crime of aggression' means the planning, preparation, initiation or execution, by a person in a position effectively to exercise control over or to direct the political or military action of a State, of an act of aggression which, by its character, gravity and scale, constitutes a manifest violation of the Charter of the United Nations.
>
> — **Rome Statute**, Art 8 bis(1)

*Why it matters:* A leadership crime with a manifest-violation threshold; the act of aggression follows Res 3314.

## What the lecturers stressed

- Opening hypothetical (Heinsch): State A invades State B to annex territory but its soldiers fully comply with IHL. Does that remove responsibility for aggression? Must the victim State comply with IHL? Who is responsible — the President, a commander, every soldier? Lesson: keep jus ad bellum and jus in bello separate. Ferencz on why States long avoided a definition.
- Progression: League Covenant (Arts 10, 12–15: cooling-off, no comprehensive ban) → Kellogg-Briand 1928 (renunciation, no enforcement) → Charter Art 2(4) with Chapter VII and Art 51. IMT Charter Art 6(a): crimes against peace (planning, preparation, initiation or waging aggressive war) — addressing resort to war, while war crimes address its conduct; a legacy of legality concerns and selectivity.
- Art 39 opens Chapter VII but does not establish individual guilt. GA Res 3314 (14 Dec 1974) defined aggression for the SC (Art 1, Art 3 list); Art 8 bis(2) imports it into criminal law.
- Road to Kampala: Rome 1998 (in Art 5 but undefined) → Preparatory Commission → Special Working Group 2002–09 → Kampala Review Conference 2010, adopted by consensus (Annex I amendments incl. Art 25(3 bis); Annex II Elements; Annex III Understandings 6 and 7). Activation conditions: 30 ratifications (Palestine the 30th, 29 June 2016; now 45), after 1 Jan 2017, ASP decision 14 Dec 2017; active from 17 July 2018.
- Elements: planning, preparation, initiation or execution (no need to fire a weapon); the completed crime needs a committed State act of aggression. Leadership is functional — effective control over or direction of a State's political or military action; title alone neither proves nor is required (industrialists or religious leaders could qualify). Art 25(3 bis) limits all modes of liability to leaders.
- Should soldiers be liable? Dannenbaum: the wrong includes unjustified killing, supporting protection for those who refuse to fight; soldiers remain responsible for their own war crimes whichever side.
- Non-state actors: Art 8 bis requires force by a State against a State; independent armed groups don't qualify; State-linked groups fall under (2)(g); the individual must still meet the leadership test.
- Manifest violation: character (nature and legal context), gravity (seriousness and consequences), scale (magnitude and duration), assessed objectively and together. Understanding 7: 'no one component can be significant enough to satisfy the manifest standard by itself'; Understanding 6: consider all circumstances. Borderline cases (brief incursions, disputed legal bases, humanitarian motives) fall below; State responsibility may remain.
- Mental element: Art 30 intent and knowledge; awareness of the facts establishing Charter inconsistency and the manifest violation (Elements 4 and 6); the accused need not have evaluated the law correctly; 'manifest' is objective.
- Kampala's trade-off: agreement on a fourth core crime and an SC compromise, but legal-certainty concerns (a definition built on Res 3314 plus 'manifest') under Art 22's strict construction.
- Jurisdiction: Art 15 bis (State referral, proprio motu) — non-parties excluded (15 bis(5)), and the 2017 activation resolution excludes States Parties that have not accepted the amendments; opt-out under 15 bis(4); the Prosecutor must first check for an SC determination and notify the SG; without one in six months the Pre-Trial Division may authorise (15 bis(8)); Art 16 deferral remains; external determinations do not bind the Court. Art 15 ter: SC referral without those consent limits, but subject to veto politics.

## Cases and materials

### IMT Nuremberg — crimes against peace
*IMT · 1946*

'To initiate a war of aggression is not only an international crime; it is the supreme international crime, differing only from other war crimes in that it contains within itself the accumulated evil of the whole.'

**Use it for:** The historical foundation and gravity.

### Kampala amendments and the 2017 ASP activation
*ICC ASP · 2010 / 2017*

Defined the crime and act of aggression and set the consent-based jurisdiction regime. The 2017 activation confirmed that the Court has no jurisdiction over nationals or territory of States parties that haven't accepted the amendments.

**Use it for:** The jurisdictional limits; why Ukraine needs a special tribunal.

### Special session on the review of the aggression amendments (Res ICC-ASP/S-1/Res.1)
*ICC ASP (NY, 7–9 July 2025) · 2025*

No consensus to harmonise Art 15 bis with the other crimes (removing the non-party exclusion and opt-out). States committed to 'strengthening' jurisdiction, annexed the proposal and convened a new special session in 2029.

**Use it for:** Why the Art 15 bis(5) gap persists; justification for the Special Tribunal.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>Open the questions (answers are folded under each)</summary>

**Q. What are the elements of the crime of aggression (Art 8 bis)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Conduct: planning, preparation, initiation or execution. Perpetrator: a leader effectively able to control or direct State political or military action. An act of aggression by a State, which by its character, gravity and scale is a manifest violation of the Charter. Mental element: awareness of the factual circumstances.

</details>

**Q. Why can't the ICC try Russian leaders for aggression against Ukraine?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Art 15 bis(5) excludes nationals of non-States parties under State referral and proprio motu triggers. An SC referral (Art 15 ter) is blocked by Russia's veto.

</details>

**Q. What is the 'manifest violation' threshold?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Character, gravity and scale must together justify a manifest designation. It filters out legally contested uses of force (e.g. arguable self-defence or humanitarian intervention).

</details>

**Q. Should ordinary soldiers be liable for aggression?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The current law says no (leadership element). Dannenbaum argues soldiers in an aggressive war have their own moral and legal claims (selective conscientious objection, recognition as victims). The traditional equality of belligerents (jus in bello) separates conduct from the legality of war.

</details>

**Q. What does Understanding 7 add to the 'manifest violation' test?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Character, gravity and scale must together justify a 'manifest' determination, and 'no one component can be significant enough to satisfy the manifest standard by itself' — a combined, objective assessment.

</details>

**Q. When did ICC jurisdiction over aggression become active, and how?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

30 ratifications reached on 29 June 2016 (Palestine); the ASP decided on 14 December 2017 after the 1 January 2017 threshold; jurisdiction active from 17 July 2018. 45 States have ratified the amendments.

</details>

**Q. Can the victim of aggression ignore IHL?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No. Jus in bello applies equally to all parties regardless of who started the war (the equality of belligerents; API preamble). Compliance with IHL also does not cure an unlawful resort to force.

</details>

**Q. How does the Ukraine Special Tribunal deal with immunity and absent accused?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Functional immunity does not apply (Art 23(4)). For a sitting Head of State, Head of Government or Foreign Minister, investigation and an indictment are possible, but confirmation is suspended until they leave office or immunity is waived (Art 23(5), 25(2)); no measures of constraint (Art 24(4)). Trials in absentia are allowed under strict conditions, with a right to retrial (Art 28(3)). The Tribunal is grounded in Ukraine's territorial jurisdiction (Art 1), delegated via a CoE treaty.

</details>
