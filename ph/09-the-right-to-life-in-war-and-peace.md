# Lecture 13: The right to life in war and peace

**Mind map:** [The right to life in war and peace](https://tomasemilioeee-a11y.github.io/pil-humanity-study-guide/mind-maps/right-to-life.html), interactive, with recall mode and search.

## Rules to know cold

1. ICCPR Art 6: no one shall be arbitrarily deprived of life. HRC GC 36: arbitrariness includes unlawfulness, inappropriateness, injustice, lack of predictability and due process, and unreasonableness, necessity and proportionality.
2. Peacetime use of lethal force: only as a last resort, to protect life or prevent serious injury from an imminent threat. Strict necessity and proportionality (GC 36 para 12; the UN Basic Principles on Force and Firearms, Principle 9).
3. ECHR Art 2(2): force 'absolutely necessary' for the listed aims. McCann (Gibraltar): the soldiers' honest belief was justified, but the planning and control of the operation (letting the suspects enter, reliance on assumptions, shooting to kill) violated Art 2.
4. Positive obligations: a legal framework, operational measures (Osman), and the procedural duty to investigate (effective, independent, prompt, transparent, with victim involvement). It applies in armed conflict (Al-Skeini; Jaloud; GC 36 para 64; Minnesota Protocol). Armed groups controlling territory may also have to investigate (Niyo).
5. Death penalty: limited to the 'most serious crimes' (intentional killing). Abolitionist trend (Second Optional Protocol; ECHR Protocols 6 and 13). Non-refoulement where there is a real risk of the death penalty (Judge v Canada).
6. In armed conflict: practices consistent with IHL are generally not arbitrary (Nuclear Weapons para 25; GC 36 para 64). But GC 36 adds IHRL considerations, such as investigating, and nuclear weapons being incompatible with Art 6.
7. IHL targeting rules: distinction (combatants and military objectives versus civilians and civilian objects); civilians lose protection only for such time as they take a direct part in hostilities (API 51(3)). The ICRC Interpretive Guidance on DPH sets three cumulative criteria: threshold of harm, direct causation, and belligerent nexus. Members of organised armed groups with a continuous combat function are targetable at all times.
8. Proportionality (API 51(5)(b)): expected incidental civilian harm must not be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. Precautions (API 57): feasible verification, choice of means, warnings, and cancelling attacks.
9. Persons hors de combat (API 41: surrender, wounded, in the power of the adversary) must not be attacked. Debate over the right to life of combatants: Rosenzweig, and Pictet's 'capture rather than kill' (least harmful means; the ICRC Guidance section IX).
10. The 2025 boat strikes (US strikes on alleged drug boats in the Caribbean and Pacific since 2 Sept 2025): there is no armed conflict with cartels (no Tadić intensity or organisation), so the IHRL law-enforcement paradigm applies, making them extrajudicial executions. States have a duty to investigate (Schmitt and Milanovic; O'Connell). This is a contemporary illustration of classification determining the applicable standard. Dated facts: the 2 Oct 2025 notice to Congress claimed a NIAC with 'unlawful combatants'; Türk (31 Oct 2025) found IHRL breaches; UN experts (Nov 2025) flagged possible international crimes; Congress inquired into the 2 Sept 'second strike' on survivors (Dec 2025); at least 223 killed in 68+ strikes by Sept 2026.
11. A 'right to survival'? Traditionally Art 6 ICCPR / Art 2 ECHR protect against killings, but there is a growing socio-economic dimension: CRC Art 6 links life with 'survival and development'; the IACtHR reads life as a right to a dignified existence; the African Commission (Ogoniland, SERAC v Nigeria) found environmental destruction violated the right to life; the ECtHR found a violation where emergency treatment was refused for inability to pay (Mehmet Şentürk and Bekir Şentürk v Turkey) but is reluctant to go further (Cyprus v Turkey left general health care open). Bantekas and Oette conclude States must take steps reasonably expected to ensure survival, including against environmental degradation (now GC 36 and the climate cases).
12. Capture before killing? Human rights courts have not required government forces in an armed conflict to try to arrest a fighter before using lethal force, but in lower-intensity counter-terrorism operations they insist on arrest rather than killing where feasible (Bantekas and Oette). This maps onto the paradigm split: conduct of hostilities versus law enforcement, and onto the ICRC Interpretive Guidance section IX debate.

## Key passages

> The test of what is an arbitrary deprivation of life, however, then falls to be determined by the applicable lex specialis, namely, the law applicable in armed conflict which is designed to regulate the conduct of hostilities.
>
> — **Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons**, ICJ Advisory Opinion (1996), para 25

*Why it matters:* ICCPR Art 6 applies in armed conflict, read through IHL; compare HRC GC 36 para 64 and Hassan.

## What the lecturers stressed

- The right to life protects against arbitrary taking; it is non-derogable but not absolute (Weeramantry: 'the irreducible core of human rights'). Provisions: ECHR Art 2, ICCPR Art 6, ACHR Art 4 (life 'in general, from the moment of conception'), ACHPR Art 4 ('human beings are inviolable').
- Targeted killing is undefined in IL. Alston's definition: intentional, premeditated and deliberate use of lethal force by States or agents under colour of law, or by an organised armed group in armed conflict, against a specific individual not in the perpetrator's custody. Unlike 'extrajudicial execution', 'summary execution' or 'assassination' (illegal by definition), targeted killing may be lawful in armed conflict.
- The answer depends on the framework. Law enforcement: lethal force only where absolutely necessary to protect persons from unlawful violence, a last resort when arrest is impossible, assessed case by case. Armed conflict: 'lethal force is the name of the game' — lawfulness turns on the target's status (combatant, fighter, DPH). 'War on terror' rhetoric is used to push the IHL framework.
- ECHR Art 2(2): force no more than absolutely necessary for (a) defence from unlawful violence, (b) lawful arrest or preventing escape, (c) quelling a riot or insurrection. Kretzmer's two prongs: is force required at all, or could other measures protect the threatened persons? If so, is lethal force needed, or would lesser force do?
- McCann (1995): an honest belief perceived for good reasons to be valid can justify force even if mistaken; but the decision not to stop the suspects entering Gibraltar, the failure to allow for intelligence errors, and the 'automatic recourse to lethal force' meant the killings were not absolutely necessary (para 213). The operation must be planned and controlled to minimise recourse to lethal force.
- Tagayeva v Russia (Beslan, 2017): a real and immediate risk to schoolchildren was known; preventive measures were inadequate (Osman duty), the legal framework on lethal force was deficient, and the rescue operation's planning and control violated Art 2.
- Strict necessity: Giuliani and Gaggio v Italy (2011) — 'absolute necessity' is stricter than 'necessary in a democratic society'. UN Basic Principles on Force and Firearms: restraint, proportionality to the seriousness of the offence, minimise damage, preserve life. IACHR Report on Terrorism: lethal force against suspects only where strictly unavoidable to protect from an imminent threat of death or serious injury.
- Alston (paras 32–33): under IHRL a killing is lawful only if required to protect life (proportionality) and there is no other means such as capture or incapacitation (necessity). So an intentional, premeditated killing by law enforcement can never be lawful; a 'shoot-to-kill' policy violates IHRL.
- In armed conflict a targeted killing is lawful only against combatants, civilians directly participating, or members with a continuous combat function, with distinction, proportionality and precautions. PCATI v Israel (2006) para 40: 'a rule-of-law state employs, to the extent possible, procedures of law and not procedures of force'. US practice blurs jus ad bellum (self-defence) and jus in bello.
- Combatant status builds up in layers: armed forces; Hague Regs Art 1 (militias and volunteer corps with responsible command, a fixed distinctive sign, open arms, respect for the laws of war); Art 2 levée en masse (spontaneous resistance to invading troops, arms carried openly, respect for the laws of war — debated for armed Ukrainian citizens in 2022); GCIII Art 4A (forces of unrecognised governments, organised resistance movements). Civilians are defined negatively (API Art 50); in doubt, a person is a civilian; the presence of non-civilians does not change the population's civilian character.
- DPH (API 51(3), APII 13(3)): 'unless and for such time'. ICRC Interpretive Guidance (2009): threshold of harm, direct causation (including as an integral part of a coordinated operation), belligerent nexus. Continuous combat function: lasting integration into an organised armed group with an ongoing role preparing, executing or commanding hostile acts; recruitment, training and equipping can prove it; political, financial or propaganda roles do not; targetable while the function lasts, without combatant privilege.
- Classroom case: Maya, a civilian, sends the precise coordinates of an enemy artillery unit at one party's request for an immediate attack — all three DPH criteria are met. Posting general support on social media is not DPH.
- Drones and globalised targeted killing: which paradigm (armed conflict or law enforcement), compliance with IHL, the 'joystick mentality', what precautions are 'feasible'. Discussion film: 'Eye in the Sky' (the trolley dilemma).

## Cases and materials

### McCann v UK
*ECtHR (GC) · 1995*

The killing of three IRA suspects in Gibraltar violated Art 2 because of failures in the control and organisation of the operation, even though the soldiers believed in good faith that it was necessary.

**Use it for:** Absolute necessity; planning and control; the duty to investigate.

### HRC General Comment 36
*UN HRC · 2018*

A broad understanding of the right to life: the duty to investigate, positive obligations (environment, climate), extraterritorial application by impact (para 63), co-application with IHL (para 64), and the death penalty.

**Use it for:** The main IHRL source for any lethal force question.

### Jaloud v Netherlands
*ECtHR (GC) · 2014*

The Netherlands had jurisdiction over a checkpoint in Iraq under its command, and the investigation of the shooting of a civilian was inadequate (Art 2 procedural).

**Use it for:** The investigation duty in armed conflict; jurisdiction through checkpoints.

### Targeted Killings case (PCATI v Israel)
*Israeli Supreme Court · 2006*

Civilians who directly participate in hostilities lose protection for such time; there must be reliable information, a less harmful means must be used if available (arrest), and there must be a retroactive investigation.

**Use it for:** DPH; the least harmful means.

### Tagayeva v Russia (Beslan)
*ECtHR · 2017*

Russia violated Art 2: it failed to take preventive measures despite specific knowledge of a real and immediate risk to schoolchildren, had a deficient legal framework on the use of force, and the rescue operation (thermobaric weapons, tanks) was not planned and controlled to minimise loss of life.

**Use it for:** Positive obligations and planning of operations.

### Giuliani and Gaggio v Italy
*ECtHR (GC) · 2011*

Shooting of a protester at the Genoa G8: the officer acted in self-defence, but the Court restated that 'absolutely necessary' requires a stricter, more compelling test than 'necessary in a democratic society'.

**Use it for:** The strictness of Art 2(2).

## Test yourself

<details><summary>Open the questions (answers are folded under each)</summary>

**Q. When may State agents use lethal force in peacetime?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Only when strictly unavoidable to protect life from an imminent threat, as a last resort, proportionate, with planning to minimise recourse to lethal force, followed by an effective investigation (GC 36; Basic Principles; McCann).

</details>

**Q. What are the three ICRC criteria for direct participation in hostilities?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Threshold of harm (likely to adversely affect military operations or cause death, injury or destruction); direct causation (one causal step); belligerent nexus (specifically designed to support a party to the detriment of another).

</details>

**Q. How does GC 36 deal with armed conflict?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Art 6 continues to apply. Uses of lethal force consistent with IHL and other applicable IL norms are, in general, not arbitrary, but States must still investigate potentially unlawful deaths and disclose criteria (para 64).

</details>

**Q. What are the elements of a proportionality assessment in attack?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Expected incidental loss of civilian life, injury, or damage to civilian objects, weighed against the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. Unlawful if excessive (API 51(5)(b), 57(2)(a)(iii); Rome 8(2)(b)(iv) 'clearly excessive').

</details>

**Q. Why do the 2025 boat strikes matter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Classification determines the law. Absent an armed conflict, IHRL law enforcement standards apply, under which the strikes are unlawful killings. Calling cartels 'unlawful combatants' cannot create an armed conflict. It also triggers the duty to investigate.

</details>

**Q. What are Kretzmer's two prongs for lethal force under Art 2?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) Is the use of force absolutely required, or could other measures protect the threatened persons? (2) If force is required, is it absolutely necessary to use lethal force, or would a lesser degree suffice?

</details>

**Q. Why can a targeted killing never be lawful under IHRL (Alston)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Lethal force is lawful only when strictly and directly necessary to save life from an imminent threat, with no alternative such as capture. In a law-enforcement operation killing can never be the sole objective; a premeditated killing plan, or a shoot-to-kill policy, is therefore unlawful.

</details>

**Q. When does a civilian with a continuous combat function lose protection, and what don't they get?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

While integrated in an organised armed group with an ongoing role preparing, executing or commanding hostile acts, they are targetable at any time, including between operations. They have no combatant privilege or POW status and can be prosecuted for participating.

</details>
